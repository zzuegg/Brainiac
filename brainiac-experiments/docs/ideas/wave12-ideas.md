**Format.** Wave 12 keeps wave 11's structure (checks, summary, one section per work package, dependency notes), with each item as a short block:

- **Block**: the reusable class, in `io.github.zzuegg.brainiac.experimental.<pkg>`.
- **Experiment**: the runnable measurement, in `io.github.zzuegg.brainiac.experiments.<theme>`.
- **Toy task**, **Baselines** (all already in the repo) and **Expected metric** (the number to report at the ~10 s test budget, with the hypothesis to test).

### 12.1.1 Lasso and elastic net by coordinate descent with warm-started paths, and LARS (Tibshirani, JRSS-B 1996; Zou & Hastie, JRSS-B 2005; Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani, J. Stat. Softw. 2010; Efron et al., Annals 2004)
The lasso adds λ‖β‖₁ to least squares and sets coefficients exactly to zero; the elastic net mixes in a ridge term so that correlated features enter together. glmnet solves the whole path over a decreasing λ grid by cyclic coordinate descent with soft-thresholding, warm starts and strong-rule screening, and picks λ by K-fold CV (λ_min or the one-standard-error rule). LARS computes the exact piecewise-linear lasso path by moving along the equiangular direction of the active set. A public block replaces the private `lassoBic` helper for new code (Lingam keeps its own copy in this wave).
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.PenalizedRegression` (LASSO, ELASTIC_NET, path with strong rules, CV with the 1-SE rule, BIC/EBIC; Gaussian and logistic) and `experimental.stats.Lars`.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.statistics.SparseRegression`.
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns` with s = 5–20, ρ ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.9} Toeplitz correlation, SNR 1–5, p up to 1000.
- **Baselines:** OLS (core `LeastSquares.ols` if landed, else `discovery.LeastSquares`), ridge, forward stepwise, `Lingam.lassoBic` (read-only), SINDy's STLSQ.
- **Expected metric:** test MSE, support F1 and path time. Hypothesis: lasso + CV beats ridge on test MSE when s ≪ p and loses at ρ = 0.9 where elastic net wins; the 1-SE rule improves support F1 over λ_min; strong rules cut path time ≥ 3× with identical solutions; LARS and coordinate descent agree to 1e-6.
- **Source:** [arXiv:math/0406456](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0406456)

### 12.1.2 Non-convex and structured penalties: SCAD, MCP, adaptive lasso and the group lasso (Fan & Li, JASA 2001; Zhang, Annals 2010; Zou, JASA 2006; Yuan & Lin, JRSS-B 2006)
The lasso's shrinkage biases large coefficients and its support recovery needs an irrepresentable condition. SCAD and MCP are folded-concave penalties whose derivative goes to zero for large |β|, giving nearly unbiased estimates and the oracle property; they are solved by coordinate descent with closed-form thresholding. The adaptive lasso reweights by 1/|β̂_init|^γ. The group lasso penalises Σ_g √p_g ‖β_g‖₂ so that whole groups (one-hot categories, polynomial expansions) enter or leave together, solved by block coordinate descent.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.PenalizedRegression` (SCAD, MCP, ADAPTIVE_LASSO, GROUP_LASSO, SPARSE_GROUP_LASSO).
- **Experiment:** `SparseRegression`.
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns` with large and small true coefficients mixed, and a grouped design (20 groups of 5, 3 active).
- **Baselines:** 12.1.1's lasso and elastic net; `NeuralGranger`'s group-lasso prox (read-only, as a reference implementation).
- **Expected metric:** coefficient estimation error ‖β̂ − β‖₂, support F1 and false positives. Hypothesis: MCP and SCAD halve the estimation error of the lasso on large coefficients and reach higher support F1 at the same λ selection rule; group lasso recovers whole groups where the lasso picks scattered members.
- **Source:** Fan & Li (JASA 2001); Zhang, "Nearly unbiased variable selection under minimax concave penalty" (Annals 2010).

### 12.1.3 Gaussian graphical models: the graphical lasso and neighbourhood selection (Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani, Biostatistics 2008; Meinshausen & Bühlmann, Annals 2006)
The conditional-independence graph of a Gaussian is the support of its precision matrix. The graphical lasso maximises log det Θ − tr(SΘ) − λ‖Θ‖₁ by block coordinate descent with a lasso per column; neighbourhood selection regresses each variable on the others with the lasso and combines the supports by AND/OR; both pick λ by EBIC or stability (12.1.5).
- **Block:** `experimental.testing.GraphicalLasso` (GLASSO, NEIGHBOURHOOD_AND, NEIGHBOURHOOD_OR, EBIC tuning), using 12.1.1's solver.
- **Experiment:** `SparseRegression` (graph table).
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns` precision matrices: chain, hub, random (p = 30–100, n = 50–400).
- **Baselines:** thresholded partial correlations from the inverse sample covariance; `Reconciliation`'s Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage inverse; the PC skeleton (`PcAlgorithm` with Fisher-z tests).
- **Expected metric:** edge F1 and Frobenius error of Θ̂. Hypothesis: glasso and neighbourhood selection beat thresholded inverses when n < 2p; the PC skeleton matches them on chains and loses on hubs.
- **Source:** [arXiv:math/0608017](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0608017)

### 12.1.4 Multiple testing and online FDR: Bonferroni/Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg/Yekutieli, Storey's adaptive BH, LORD++ and SAFFRON, and e-value online FDR with e-LORD/e-SAFFRON (Benjamini & Hochberg, JRSS-B 1995; Benjamini & Yekutieli, Annals 2001; Storey, JRSS-B 2002; Ramdas et al., ICML 2018; Zhang et al., ICML 2025)
BH rejects the k smallest p-values with p_(k) ≤ kα/m, controlling the false discovery rate under independence and PRDS; BY adds a log m factor for arbitrary dependence; Storey estimates the null proportion π₀ for more power. Online FDR tests a stream of hypotheses in order with an α-wealth: LORD++ spends wealth that is refilled by rejections, SAFFRON adapts to π₀. e-GAI (2025) works with e-values, sets each level from previous rejections and costs while spending less wealth per rejection, which keeps FDR control under more general dependence and gives e-LORD and e-SAFFRON. e-BH is the batch e-value counterpart.
- **Block:** `experimental.testing.MultipleTesting` (BONFERRONI, HOLM, BH, BY, STOREY_BH, E_BH) and `experimental.testing.OnlineFdr` (LOND, LORD_PLUS_PLUS, SAFFRON, E_LORD, E_SAFFRON).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.statistics.MultipleTesting`.
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns` p-value streams: m = 1000 tests, π₀ ∈ {0.8, 0.95, 0.99}, independent and block-correlated z-statistics; signals clustered early or late in the stream; e-values from likelihood ratios; plus a real use: per-feature t-tests on `TabularStressSuite` (core `Distributions` t if landed).
- **Baselines:** uncorrected testing; Bonferroni and Holm.
- **Expected metric:** empirical FDR (over 200 replications) and power. Hypothesis: BH holds FDR ≤ α and has 2–5× Bonferroni's power; Storey adds power at π₀ = 0.8; under strong correlation BY and e-BH keep control where BH's power is unchanged; online, SAFFRON beats LORD++ when signals are dense and e-LORD keeps FDR under dependence where LORD++ exceeds it slightly.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1802.09098](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09098), [arXiv:2009.02824](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02824), [arXiv:2506.01452](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01452)

### 12.1.5 Variable selection with error control: model-X knockoffs and stability selection (Candès, Fan, Janson & Lv, JRSS-B 2018; Barber & Candès, Annals 2015; Meinshausen & Bühlmann, JRSS-B 2010)
Knockoffs build a fake copy X̃ of the features that has the same correlation structure but no link to y (for Gaussian X, by solving for the equicorrelated or SDP diagonal), fit a lasso on [X, X̃], and compare each feature with its knockoff by the statistic W_j = |β_j| − |β̃_j|; the knockoff+ threshold controls FDR exactly in finite samples. Stability selection refits the lasso on many half-samples and keeps features selected in more than a fraction π_thr of fits, with a bound on expected false selections.
- **Block:** `experimental.testing.Knockoffs` (Gaussian model-X, equicorrelated and SDP-by-coordinate-ascent constructions, lasso-coefficient-difference statistic, knockoff and knockoff+ thresholds) and `experimental.testing.StabilitySelection`.
- **Experiment:** `MultipleTesting`.
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns`, n = 400, p = 100–200, s = 20, ρ ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.6}.
- **Baselines:** lasso + CV support (12.1.1); BH on marginal correlation p-values (12.1.4); BH on OLS p-values when n > p.
- **Expected metric:** FDR and power at q = 0.1, over 50 replications. Hypothesis: knockoff+ holds FDR ≤ 0.1 at every ρ while lasso-CV has FDR 0.3–0.5; marginal BH loses control at ρ = 0.6 (correlated nulls); stability selection has the lowest FDR and the lowest power.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1610.02351](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.02351), [arXiv:0809.2932](https://arxiv.org/abs/0809.2932)

### 12.1.6 Conformal novelty detection with FDR control: split-conformal p-values with BH, and full-conformal e-values with e-BH (Bates, Candès, Lei, Romano & Sesia, Annals 2023; Lee, Popov & Ren, Jan 2025)
Anomaly detectors output scores, not error rates. Split-conformal novelty detection calibrates any score on held-out inliers to a p-value p_j = (1 + #{calibration scores ≥ s_j}) / (n + 1); these p-values are PRDS, so BH over a test batch controls the FDR of the flagged points. The 2025 full-conformal method trains the score on all reference data (no split), turns full-conformal scores into e-values and applies e-BH, which keeps finite-sample FDR control without PRDS and, in the paper, gives more power when reference data are scarce; a K-block variant trains one score per test block.
- **Block:** `experimental.testing.ConformalNovelty` (SPLIT_BH, SPLIT_STOREY, FULL_E_BH, K_BLOCK_E_BH), taking any `ScoreFunction`.
- **Experiment:** `MultipleTesting` (novelty table).
- **Toy task:** tabular anomalies from `TabularStressSuite` and digit novelty from `ToyTasks` (one class held out), with 1–10% outliers per test batch and reference sets of 100–1000 inliers.
- **Baselines:** `IsolationForest` and `Ecod` scores thresholded at a fixed top-5% or at a calibrated quantile without multiplicity correction.
- **Expected metric:** realised FDR and power at q = 0.1. Hypothesis: fixed thresholds give FDR 0.3–0.8 when outliers are rare, conformal BH holds ≤ 0.1; full-conformal e-BH beats split BH in power at 100 reference points and ties at 1000.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2104.08279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08279), [arXiv:2501.02703](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.02703)

### 12.1.7 Robust regression and covariance: Huber and Tukey M-estimators, least trimmed squares, Theil–Sen, the minimum covariance determinant and median-of-means (Huber, Annals 1964; Rousseeuw & Van Driessen, DMKD 2006 and Technometrics 1999; Sen, JASA 1968; Lugosi & Mendelson, 2019)
OLS breaks with one bad point. M-estimators down-weight large residuals by iteratively reweighted least squares (Huber's ψ is monotone; Tukey's bisquare redescends to zero), with the scale from the MAD. FAST-LTS fits the h-subset with the smallest residual sum of squares by concentration steps from random starts (breakdown 50%); Theil–Sen takes the median of pairwise slopes. FAST-MCD finds the h-subset with the smallest covariance determinant, giving robust Mahalanobis distances. Median-of-means splits the data into blocks and takes the median of block means, giving sub-Gaussian error bounds under heavy tails.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.RobustRegression` (HUBER, TUKEY, LTS, THEIL_SEN, MM) and `experimental.stats.RobustCovariance` (MCD, MEDIAN_OF_MEANS for means and gradients).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.statistics.RegressionModels`.
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns` low-dimensional (p = 5–20) with 0–40% vertical outliers and leverage points, and t₂ noise; outlier detection by robust distances.
- **Baselines:** OLS (`LeastSquares.ols`); the wave 11 RANSAC in `FeatureMatching` (line and plane fitting); empirical covariance.
- **Expected metric:** coefficient error vs contamination share, and outlier-detection AUROC. Hypothesis: Huber holds to about 10% vertical outliers but breaks with leverage points; LTS and MM hold to 40%; MCD distances detect leverage outliers with AUROC > 0.95 where classical Mahalanobis masks them.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1906.04280](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.04280); Rousseeuw & Van Driessen, "Computing LTS regression for large data sets" (DMKD 2006).

### 12.1.8 Linear quantile regression and non-crossing quantiles (Koenker & Bassett, Econometrica 1978; Chernozhukov, Fernández-Val & Galichon, Econometrica 2010)
Quantile regression minimises the pinball loss Σ ρ_τ(y − xᵀβ), a linear program; solving it exactly (simplex over `LinearProgram`) or by IRLS gives conditional quantiles that are robust to outliers in y. Fitting several τ separately can make quantile lines cross; monotone rearrangement sorts the predicted quantiles per point, which provably reduces the error.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.QuantileRegression` (LP via `LinearProgram`, IRLS, a penalised ℓ₁ version reusing 12.1.1, rearrangement).
- **Experiment:** `RegressionModels`.
- **Toy task:** heteroscedastic linear data with skewed noise; `TabularStressSuite` regression columns; crossing stress with few samples.
- **Baselines:** `QuantileForest`; GBT quantile loss (`BoostLosses`); CQR intervals (`ConformalForecaster`).
- **Expected metric:** pinball loss per τ, interval coverage at 80/90%, crossing rate. Hypothesis: on linear heteroscedastic data linear QR beats QRF in pinball loss with 200 points; rearrangement removes all crossings and never increases pinball loss; QRF wins on nonlinear columns.
- **Source:** [arXiv:0704.3649](https://arxiv.org/abs/0704.3649)

### 12.1.9 Penalised splines, additive models and MARS (Eilers & Marx, Statistical Science 1996; Hastie & Tibshirani, 1990; Wood, JRSS-B 2011; Friedman, Annals 1991)
P-splines use a rich B-spline basis with a difference penalty on adjacent coefficients, with λ chosen by GCV (or REML); a generalised additive model sums one smooth per feature and fits them by backfitting or by one penalised least squares system. MARS builds a model from hinge-function products with a greedy forward pass and a GCV backward pruning pass, giving interactions with few terms.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.PenalizedSplines` (P-spline smoother, GCV), `experimental.stats.AdditiveModel` (backfitting, logistic GAM via P-IRLS) and `experimental.stats.Mars`.
- **Experiment:** `RegressionModels`.
- **Toy task:** Friedman #1 and #3 functions, additive sine/step components with noise, and `TabularStressSuite`'s additive columns.
- **Baselines:** `ExplainableBoosting` (cyclic GA²M); `KanLayer` networks; polynomial ridge; GBT with monotone constraints where a shape is monotone (core GBT option if landed).
- **Expected metric:** test RMSE and fit time; per-feature shape error against the true components. Hypothesis: GAMs match EBM's accuracy on additive data at 10–100× less fit time and recover smoother shapes; MARS beats both on Friedman #1 because of its interaction term.
- **Source:** Eilers & Marx, "Flexible smoothing with B-splines and penalties" (Statistical Science 1996); Friedman, "Multivariate adaptive regression splines" (Annals 1991).

### 12.1.10 Ordinal regression: proportional odds, CORAL and CORN (McCullagh, JRSS-B 1980; Cao, Mirjalili & Raschka, Pattern Recognition Letters 2020; Shi, Cao & Raschka, Pattern Analysis and Applications 2023)
Ordered labels (ratings, severity grades) are badly served by softmax, which ignores order, and by regression, which assumes equal spacing. The proportional-odds model has one score and K − 1 ordered cut-points; CORAL turns the problem into K − 1 binary "y > k" tasks sharing weights with separate biases, which guarantees rank consistency; CORN trains the k-th task only on examples with y > k − 1 (conditional probabilities) and multiplies them, dropping the weight-sharing constraint.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.OrdinalRegression` (PROPORTIONAL_ODDS, CORAL, CORN), as heads for any MLP.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.statistics.LabelStructures`.
- **Toy task:** digits relabelled to ordinal "size" grades; `TabularStressSuite` regression targets binned into 5–10 ordered classes with unequal spacing; label noise at ±1 grade.
- **Baselines:** softmax cross-entropy; MSE regression with rounding; LDAM/logit adjustment heads are not needed.
- **Expected metric:** MAE in grades, exact accuracy and rank-consistency violations. Hypothesis: CORAL/CORN cut MAE by 10–20% over softmax at equal accuracy; regression with rounding loses under unequal spacing; softmax has non-monotone cumulative probabilities on 5–15% of inputs.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1901.07884](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.07884), [arXiv:2111.08851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.08851)

### 12.1.11 Multi-label classification: binary relevance, classifier chains, label powerset and ML-kNN (Read et al., Machine Learning 2011; Tsoumakas & Katakis, 2007; Zhang & Zhou, Pattern Recognition 2007)
With several labels per input, binary relevance trains one independent classifier per label and ignores correlations. Classifier chains feed earlier labels' predictions as features to later classifiers (ensembles average random orders); label powerset treats each observed label set as one class; ML-kNN uses neighbours' label counts in a Bayesian rule. Probabilistic chains with beam search optimise subset accuracy rather than Hamming loss.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.MultiLabel` (BINARY_RELEVANCE, CLASSIFIER_CHAIN, ENSEMBLE_CHAINS, LABEL_POWERSET, ML_KNN, beam inference).
- **Experiment:** `LabelStructures`.
- **Toy task:** multi-shape `ShapeScenes` images (which shapes are present) and a tabular task with planted label dependencies (label 2 = label 0 AND label 1 with noise).
- **Baselines:** independent sigmoid heads on one MLP; `ConformalSets`' multilabel risk-control row (wave 11) as the set baseline.
- **Expected metric:** Hamming loss, subset accuracy, micro-F1. Hypothesis: chains and label powerset beat binary relevance in subset accuracy on the dependent-label task by 5–15 points at equal Hamming loss; ML-kNN is competitive on the image task only with good features.
- **Source:** Read, Pfahringer, Holmes & Frank, "Classifier chains for multi-label classification" (Machine Learning 2011).

### 12.1.12 Imbalance resampling and its calibration cost: Borderline-SMOTE, ADASYN, Tomek links/ENN and balanced bagging (Han et al., ICIC 2005; He et al., IJCNN 2008; Tomek, 1976; Liu, Wu & Zhou, IEEE TSMC-B 2009; Liu, Jun 2026)
Oversampling interpolates minority points (SMOTE; Borderline-SMOTE only near the boundary; ADASYN in proportion to local difficulty); cleaning removes Tomek links or misclassified points (ENN); EasyEnsemble/balanced bagging undersample per member. The 2026 study finds that resampling leaves F1 and AUC flat while raising ECE (dramatically for random undersampling at high imbalance), that the prior-shift correction fixes undersampling but not SMOTE, and that post-hoc Platt/isotonic recalibration removes most of the damage.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.Resampling` (SMOTE, BORDERLINE_SMOTE, ADASYN, TOMEK, ENN, RANDOM_UNDER, EASY_ENSEMBLE, PRIOR_SHIFT_CORRECTION).
- **Experiment:** `LabelStructures`.
- **Toy task:** `TabularStressSuite` binary tasks at imbalance 1:10, 1:50, 1:200; `ToyTasks` spirals with a rare class.
- **Baselines:** class weights; wave 10 logit adjustment (`LogitAdjustment`); `SyntheticTraining`'s SMOTE-style interpolation (read-only); wave 11 `Recalibrators` for the post-hoc fix.
- **Expected metric:** AUROC, minority F1, balanced accuracy and ECE before and after recalibration. Hypothesis: resampling raises minority F1 by a few points over plain training but no more than class weights or logit adjustment; ECE rises (most for undersampling at 1:200); prior-shift correction repairs undersampling but not SMOTE; isotonic recalibration brings every method's ECE back within 0.02 of the unweighted model.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2606.29720](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29720); He et al., "ADASYN" (IJCNN 2008).

### 12.1.13 Feature selection: mRMR, ReliefF, Boruta, stochastic gates and LassoNet (Peng et al., TPAMI 2005; Kononenko, ECML 1994; Kursa & Rudnicki, J. Stat. Softw. 2010; Yamada et al., ICML 2020; Lemhadri et al., JMLR 2021)
Filters rank features by relevance minus redundancy (mRMR with mutual information) or by nearest-hit/nearest-miss differences (ReliefF, which catches interactions); Boruta compares each feature's forest importance with shuffled "shadow" copies; embedded neural methods learn a sparse input layer: stochastic gates relax ℓ₀ with clipped Gaussian gates, and LassoNet constrains each feature's hidden weights by its skip-connection coefficient so the network is sparse in inputs along a path.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.FeatureSelection` (MRMR, RELIEFF, BORUTA, STOCHASTIC_GATES, LASSONET).
- **Experiment:** `LabelStructures`.
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns` nonlinear variants (XOR pairs, sine features, 5 relevant of 100, redundant copies), and `TabularStressSuite` with 50 added noise columns.
- **Baselines:** 12.1.1's lasso path; permutation importance from `RandomForest`; KernelSHAP from `ModelAgnosticExplainer`.
- **Expected metric:** relevant-feature recall at k selected, and downstream MLP accuracy with the top-k. Hypothesis: the lasso misses XOR pairs, which ReliefF, Boruta and stochastic gates find; LassoNet and STG match Boruta's recall at 10× less time; mRMR drops redundant copies that permutation importance double-counts.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1810.04247](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04247), [arXiv:1907.12207](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.12207)

### 12.1.14 Learning rankings from comparisons: Bradley–Terry by MM, Rank Centrality, Plackett–Luce, Borda/Kemeny aggregation, and TrueSkill and Glicko-2 ratings (Hunter, Annals 2004; Negahban, Oh & Shah, Operations Research 2017; Herbrich, Minka & Graepel, NeurIPS 2006; Glickman, 2012)
Bradley–Terry's MLE by Hunter's MM updates ranks items from pairwise wins; Rank Centrality ranks by the stationary distribution of a random walk that moves to winners; Plackett–Luce generalises to full and partial rankings; Borda counts and a Kemeny local search aggregate several rankings. TrueSkill keeps a Gaussian skill per player and updates it by expectation propagation on a factor graph, so new players move fast; Glicko-2 adds a rating deviation and volatility.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.RankModels` (BRADLEY_TERRY_MM, RANK_CENTRALITY, PLACKETT_LUCE, BORDA, KEMENY_LOCAL_SEARCH) and `experimental.stats.Ratings` (TRUESKILL, GLICKO2).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.statistics.Comparisons`.
- **Toy task:** 50–200 items with planted Bradley–Terry scores; sparse comparison graphs (Erdős–Rényi with 2–10 comparisons per item); a match stream with player turnover and skill drift.
- **Baselines:** `EloLeague`'s Elo; raw win rate; the Bradley–Terry utilities in `PreferenceLosses`/`DuelingBandits` (read-only).
- **Expected metric:** Kendall τ with the true ranking vs comparisons per item; next-match log loss for the rating systems. Hypothesis: BT-MM and Rank Centrality beat win rate by 0.1–0.2 τ on sparse graphs; TrueSkill and Glicko-2 beat Elo's next-match log loss mainly in the first 10 games of new players.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1209.1688](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.1688); Herbrich, Minka & Graepel, "TrueSkill" (NeurIPS 2006).

### 12.1.15 Knowledge tracing: Bayesian knowledge tracing, DKT, simpleKT and DKT2 (Corbett & Anderson, 1994; Piech et al., NeurIPS 2015; Liu et al., ICLR 2023; Zhou, Han & Chen, 2025)
Knowledge tracing predicts whether a student will answer the next question correctly from their answer history. BKT is a two-state HMM per skill with learn, forget, slip and guess parameters fitted by EM; DKT runs an LSTM over (question, correctness) tokens; simpleKT is a strong attention baseline with Rasch-style question-difficulty embeddings; DKT2 (2025) builds Rasch embeddings, runs xLSTM blocks, and reads out knowledge by subtracting IRT difficulty from ability, split into familiar and unfamiliar parts.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.KnowledgeTracing` (BKT by EM on `HiddenMarkovModel`-style code, DKT, SIMPLE_KT, DKT2_LITE with an LSTM in place of xLSTM if the xLSTM block is unavailable).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.statistics.LearnerModelling`.
- **Toy task:** `LearnerTraces`: 200–1000 students, 20–50 skills, 100–500 questions, 50–200 answers each, with and without forgetting.
- **Baselines:** `BenchmarkMethodology`'s 2-PL IRT (static ability, read-only); per-skill running accuracy; logistic regression on counts (PFA-style).
- **Expected metric:** next-answer AUC and the correlation of inferred mastery with the true knowledge state. Hypothesis: BKT is near-optimal when data come from BKT and loses to DKT/simpleKT when knowledge transfers across skills; simpleKT matches or beats DKT; DKT2-lite's Rasch readout gives the best mastery correlation.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1506.05908](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.05908), [arXiv:2302.06881](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06881), [arXiv:2501.14256](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.14256)

### 12.1.16 Multicalibration and multiaccuracy boosting (Hébert-Johnson, Kim, Reingold & Rothblum, ICML 2018; Kim, Ghorbani & Zou, AIES 2019)
A model can be calibrated on average and badly miscalibrated on subgroups. Multicalibration asks for calibration on every group in a rich (possibly intersecting) collection and every prediction bin; the algorithm repeatedly finds a (group, bin) with a calibration violation above α and patches the predictions there, converging in O(1/α²) rounds. Multiaccuracy boosting uses a small auditor regression on residuals to find and correct groups with biased mean predictions, without knowing the groups.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.Multicalibration` (MULTICALIBRATE with explicit groups, MULTIACCURACY_BOOST with a ridge or tree auditor).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.statistics.FairCalibration`.
- **Toy task:** `TabularStressSuite` with planted subgroups (intersections of two binary attributes) whose base rates and feature–label slopes differ; wave 10's coloured-shapes task via `CausalAndFairness.colouredShapes` (read-only).
- **Baselines:** temperature scaling and isotonic recalibration (`Calibration`, `Recalibrators`); per-group temperature scaling with known groups; `GroupRobustTraining` (GroupDRO).
- **Expected metric:** worst-group ECE, overall ECE and AUROC. Hypothesis: global recalibration leaves worst-group ECE 3–5× the overall ECE; multicalibration brings it within 2× without hurting AUROC; multiaccuracy boosting closes half the gap without group labels.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1711.08513](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08513), [arXiv:1805.12317](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12317)

### 12.1.17 Generalised linear models for counts and zero-heavy outcomes: Poisson, negative binomial, zero-inflated and Tweedie GLMs (Nelder & Wedderburn, JRSS-A 1972; Lambert, Technometrics 1992; Jørgensen, 1987)
Counts and insurance-style losses violate least squares. A GLM pairs a linear predictor with a link and an exponential-family variance, fitted by IRLS with offsets for exposure; the negative binomial adds overdispersion; zero-inflated models mix a point mass at zero with a count model (fitted by EM); the Tweedie compound Poisson–gamma family (1 < p < 2) handles exact zeros plus positive continuous amounts.
- **Block:** `experimental.stats.CountGlm` (POISSON, NEGATIVE_BINOMIAL, ZERO_INFLATED_POISSON, TWEEDIE; offsets; ridge option).
- **Experiment:** `RegressionModels`.
- **Toy task:** `SparseDesigns` count outcomes with exposure, overdispersion φ ∈ {1, 3, 10} and 30–70% structural zeros; claim-cost data with a Tweedie generator.
- **Baselines:** OLS on log(1 + y); GBT with squared loss (core `GradientBoostedTrees`); an MLP with MSE.
- **Expected metric:** test deviance (Poisson/Tweedie), coverage of 90% predictive intervals, and the predicted zero share. Hypothesis: the matched GLM beats log-OLS in deviance by 10–30% and is the only one with correct zero shares; GBT with squared loss wins on nonlinear means but is miscalibrated in intervals.
- **Source:** Nelder & Wedderburn, "Generalized linear models" (JRSS-A 1972); Lambert, "Zero-inflated Poisson regression" (Technometrics 1992).

### 12.1.18 Community detection: Louvain, spectral and belief-propagation SBM inference, and GNNs with skip connections and jumping knowledge (Blondel et al., J. Stat. Mech. 2008; Decelle et al., PRE 2011; Chen, Li & Bruna, ICLR 2019; Xu et al., ICML 2018)
Louvain greedily moves nodes between communities and then contracts them to maximise modularity; spectral clustering of the (regularised or non-backtracking) adjacency recovers SBM communities above the detectability threshold; belief propagation on the SBM is optimal down to the Kesten–Stigum threshold. Supervised line-graph GNNs learn community detection from labelled SBM samples; jumping-knowledge networks combine every layer's representation (concat or max) so depth does not over-smooth. The enabling step adds an optional residual (input-to-output) skip and a JK readout to `GcnLayer` stacks (the wave 11 `QuboGnn` local skip stays where it is).

- **Experiment:** `experiments.graphs.CommunityDetection`.
- **Toy task:** `SyntheticGraphs`/`GnnBenchmarks` SBMs with 2–5 communities, n = 200–1000, sweeping the signal (c_in − c_out) across the detectability threshold; degree-corrected variants.
- **Baselines:** `SpectralClustering` on the adjacency; label propagation; a plain GCN stack of the same depth.
- **Expected metric:** overlap (NMI) vs signal-to-noise. Hypothesis: BP and non-backtracking spectral reach non-trivial overlap closest to the threshold, plain spectral fails on sparse graphs, Louvain over-splits; the GCN with skip/JK holds accuracy at depth 8 where the plain GCN collapses.
- **Source:** [arXiv:0803.0476](https://arxiv.org/abs/0803.0476), [arXiv:1109.3041](https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.3041), [arXiv:1705.08415](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08415), [arXiv:1806.03536](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.03536)

### 12.2.1 Stream clustering: sequential and mini-batch k-means, BIRCH, CluStream and DenStream (MacQueen, 1967; Sculley, WWW 2010; Zhang et al., SIGMOD 1996; Aggarwal et al., VLDB 2003; Cao et al., SDM 2006)
Batch clusterers revisit the data; stream clusterers keep a summary. Sequential k-means updates the nearest centre with a 1/n step, mini-batch k-means does the same per batch; BIRCH keeps clustering-feature (N, LS, SS) triples in a height-balanced tree; CluStream maintains q micro-clusters online and runs k-means on them offline, with pyramidal snapshots for time horizons; DenStream keeps potential and outlier micro-clusters with exponential fading, and runs DBSCAN on them, so evolving and arbitrary-shaped clusters and noise are handled.
- **Block:** `experimental.stream.StreamClustering` (SEQUENTIAL_KMEANS, MINIBATCH_KMEANS, BIRCH, CLUSTREAM, DENSTREAM).
- **Experiment:** `StreamShootout.clustering`.
- **Toy task:** `DriftingStreams`-style Gaussian blobs that appear, merge, drift and disappear (10⁴–10⁵ points, 2–10 dimensions, 5% noise), plus non-convex moons.
- **Baselines:** batch `KMeans` and `Dbscan` refit on a sliding window.
- **Expected metric:** windowed adjusted Rand index and purity over time, memory (summaries kept) and throughput. Hypothesis: DenStream tracks appearing/disappearing and non-convex clusters best, CluStream wins on Gaussian drift with 10× less memory than windowed k-means; windowed batch methods are 10–100× slower at similar ARI.
- **Source:** Sculley, "Web-scale k-means clustering" (WWW 2010); Cao, Ester, Qian & Zhou, "Density-based clustering over an evolving data stream with noise" (SDM 2006).

### 12.2.2 Streaming anomaly detection: half-space trees, robust random cut forests, LODA and Online Isolation Forest (Tan, Ting & Liu, IJCAI 2011; Guha et al., ICML 2016; Pevný, Machine Learning 2016; Leveni et al., May 2025)
Half-space trees split random dimensions at mid-ranges and score a point by mass in the leaves of a reference window that is swapped periodically. RRCF keeps a forest of random cut trees under insertion and deletion and scores by collusive displacement (how much the tree changes when the point is removed). LODA averages one-dimensional histogram densities on sparse random projections. Online-iForest (2025) grows multi-resolution histogram trees whose bins split when they fill and merge when old points leave a sliding window, scoring by average leaf depth with no retraining.
- **Block:** `experimental.stream.StreamingAnomaly` (HALF_SPACE_TREES, RRCF, LODA, ONLINE_IFOREST).
- **Experiment:** `StreamShootout.anomalies`.
- **Toy task:** `DriftingStreams` and `AnomalySeries`-style streams with point, collective and contextual anomalies (1–3%), gradual and abrupt drift.
- **Baselines:** batch `IsolationForest` retrained every W points; `Ecod` on a sliding window.
- **Expected metric:** prequential AUROC and AUPRC, points per second, and AUROC after drift. Hypothesis: Online-iForest and half-space trees match a periodically retrained isolation forest within 0.02 AUROC at 5–20× the throughput; RRCF is best on collective anomalies; LODA is fastest and weakest under drift.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2505.09593](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09593); Guha et al., "Robust random cut forest based anomaly detection on streams" (ICML 2016).

### 12.2.3 Streaming PCA with an exact Oja term: Oja's rule, CCIPCA and incremental SVD (Oja, J. Math. Biol. 1982; Weng, Zhang & Hwang, TPAMI 2003; Brand, ECCV 2002; Ross et al., IJCV 2008)
Oja's rule updates w ← w + η y (x − y w), whose stable point is the top principal component; wave 11's Frequent Directions had to write its own because `HebbianRule` has no exact Oja term (only a normalised Hebb rule). Candid covariance-free incremental PCA (CCIPCA) estimates several components with amnesic averaging and deflation and needs no learning rate; Brand's incremental SVD updates a thin SVD with each new block exactly (with forgetting). The enabling change adds `HebbianRule.oja(rate)` with the exact term; existing rules are unchanged.
- **Block:** `experimental.stream.StreamingPca` (OJA_K with QR, CCIPCA, INCREMENTAL_SVD); `HebbianRule` gains an exact Oja option (default unchanged); `FrequentDirections.oja` may switch to it in a later PR.
- **Experiment:** `StreamShootout.subspaces`.
- **Toy task:** spiked-covariance streams (d = 50–500, k = 5, eigengap 1–10) with a rotating subspace; digits pixels as a real stream.
- **Baselines:** `PlasticityRule.sanger` (GHA); wave 11 `FrequentDirections`; batch `ThinSvd` on everything seen (oracle).
- **Expected metric:** subspace distance (sin Θ) to the true or batch subspace vs points seen, and time per point. Hypothesis: incremental SVD matches the batch subspace to 1e-6 when nothing is forgotten; CCIPCA beats Oja and GHA at small eigengaps because it has no step size to tune; FD is best per byte of memory.
- **Source:** Weng, Zhang & Hwang, "Candid covariance-free incremental principal component analysis" (TPAMI 2003); Brand, "Incremental singular value decomposition of uncertain data with missing values" (ECCV 2002).

### 12.2.4 Mondrian forests for online classification and regression, with a regression mode for `RandomForest` (Lakshminarayanan, Roy & Teh, NeurIPS 2014 and AISTATS 2016)
A Mondrian process recursively splits a box at times drawn from an exponential race weighted by side lengths; Mondrian trees can be extended online exactly in distribution as new points arrive (a split can be inserted above existing ones), and predictions use hierarchical Bayesian smoothing of leaf counts (classification) or Gaussian beliefs (regression), giving calibrated uncertainty far from data. The enabling change gives `RandomForest` a regression mode (variance-reduction splits, mean leaves) so the batch baseline is the same forest code; `RegressionForest` is not changed.
- **Block:** `experimental.stream.MondrianForest` (classification and regression, online extension, lifetime budget); `RandomForest` gains `Mode.REGRESSION` (default classification, unchanged).
- **Experiment:** `StreamShootout.forests`.
- **Toy task:** `DriftingStreams` classification streams; Friedman #1 regression streamed; a gap-in-the-input test for uncertainty.
- **Baselines:** `HoeffdingTree` and `AdaptiveRandomForest`; batch `RandomForest` (classification and the new regression mode) refit at checkpoints; `RegressionForest` (read-only).
- **Expected metric:** prequential accuracy or RMSE, refit cost, and predictive NLL in the input gap. Hypothesis: Mondrian forests reach batch-RF accuracy after a few thousand points, beat Hoeffding trees early in the stream, and widen their predictive variance in the gap where RF does not.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1406.2673](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2673), [arXiv:1506.03805](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.03805)

### 12.2.5 Online kernel learning on a budget: NORMA, the Forgetron, budgeted kernel perceptrons and online random features (Kivinen, Smola & Williamson, IEEE TSP 2004; Dekel, Shalev-Shwartz & Singer, SIAM J. Comput. 2008; Wang, Crammer & Vucetic, JMLR 2012; Lu et al., JMLR 2016)
Online kernel methods grow one support vector per mistake. NORMA does kernelised SGD with shrinkage so old coefficients decay and can be truncated; the Forgetron removes the oldest support vector under a fixed budget with a provable mistake bound; budgeted SGD merges or removes support vectors (BSGD); online random Fourier features keep a fixed-size linear model on randomised features.
- **Block:** `experimental.online.BudgetedKernels` (KERNEL_PERCEPTRON, NORMA, FORGETRON, BSGD_REMOVE, BSGD_MERGE, ONLINE_RFF).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.streaming.OnlinePrediction`.
- **Toy task:** `ToyTasks` spirals and digits streamed; `DriftingStreams` with a rotating boundary; budgets of 50–500 support vectors.
- **Baselines:** `FtrlProximal` on raw features; batch `KernelSvm` (offline upper bound); online logistic regression (`OnlineLogistic`).
- **Expected metric:** online mistake rate and final accuracy vs budget, and time per point. Hypothesis: on spirals every kernel method beats linear FTRL by more than 20 points; BSGD merging is best per budget; online RFF with 500 features matches BSGD at 200 support vectors and is the fastest.
- **Source:** Kivinen, Smola & Williamson, "Online learning with kernels" (IEEE TSP 2004); Wang, Crammer & Vucetic, "Breaking the curse of kernelization" (JMLR 2012).

### 12.2.6 Prediction with expert advice: Hedge, AdaHedge, Squint and fixed share for forecast combination (Freund & Schapire, JCSS 1997; de Rooij et al., JMLR 2014; Koolen & van Erven, COLT 2015; Herbster & Warmuth, Machine Learning 1998)
Hedge keeps exponential weights over experts and has regret O(√(T log N)) at a tuned rate; AdaHedge tunes the rate from the observed mixability gap; Squint has second-order, quantile regret bounds that adapt to easy data; fixed share mixes a little weight back to all experts each round, so the combination tracks the best expert when it switches.
- **Block:** `experimental.online.ExpertAdvice` (FOLLOW_THE_LEADER, HEDGE, ADAHEDGE, SQUINT, FIXED_SHARE).
- **Experiment:** `OnlinePrediction` (forecast-combination table).
- **Toy task:** combining 6–10 forecasters (seasonal naive, ETS, DLinear-style linear models, an MLP) on `SeasonalSeries` with regime switches; a stochastic and an adversarial synthetic expert set.
- **Baselines:** best single expert in hindsight; uniform averaging; follow-the-leader.
- **Expected metric:** cumulative loss and regret vs the best expert and vs the best switching sequence. Hypothesis: AdaHedge and Squint match tuned Hedge with no tuning; fixed share wins after regime switches; simple averaging is hard to beat on stationary series (the "forecast combination puzzle").
- **Source:** [arXiv:1301.0534](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.0534); Herbster & Warmuth, "Tracking the best expert" (Machine Learning 1998).

### 12.2.7 Quantile sketches: Greenwald–Khanna, KLL and t-digest (Greenwald & Khanna, SIGMOD 2001; Karnin, Lang & Liberty, FOCS 2016; Dunning & Ertl, 2019)
Wave 11 added frequency and distinct-count sketches; streaming quantiles (latency percentiles, feature binning for streaming GBDTs) need rank sketches. GK keeps tuples with rank bounds and merges them deterministically to ε-rank error; KLL keeps compactors of geometrically decreasing capacity that randomly drop half of each sorted pair, with optimal space; t-digest clusters points with a scale function that keeps tail clusters small, giving very accurate extreme quantiles.
- **Block:** `experimental.sketch.QuantileSketches` (GREENWALD_KHANNA, KLL, T_DIGEST; mergeable).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.algorithms.SketchShootout.quantiles` (a new method in wave 11's class; its rows stay unchanged).
- **Toy task:** `KeyStreams` value streams: uniform, log-normal, Pareto and sorted (adversarial) orders, 10⁶ items; merging 16 partial sketches.
- **Baselines:** exact sort (ground truth); reservoir sampling of the same memory (`ReservoirReplay` sampling logic); a fixed-width histogram.
- **Expected metric:** rank error at q ∈ {0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999} vs memory. Hypothesis: KLL has the best rank error per byte at mid quantiles, t-digest the best relative error at 0.999, GK is deterministic but needs 2–3× the space; reservoir sampling is 3–10× worse at the tails.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1603.05346](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05346), [arXiv:1902.04023](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04023)

### 12.2.8 Conformal PID control for online prediction intervals (Angelopoulos, Candès & Tibshirani, NeurIPS 2023)
ACI adjusts the miscoverage level by the last error, which oscillates under trends. Conformal PID control views interval calibration as control: quantile tracking (the P term) updates the score quantile directly by the coverage error; an integrator (I) adds the accumulated error with a saturation function for long-run guarantees; a scorecaster (D-like) forecasts the next score with any model. The guarantee is long-run coverage for arbitrary sequences.
- **Block:** `experimental.online.ConformalPid` (QUANTILE_TRACKING, PI, PID with a linear scorecaster).
- **Experiment:** `OnlinePrediction` (intervals table).
- **Toy task:** `SeasonalSeries` with a level shift, a variance burst and a trend; `ChaoticSeries` one-step forecasts; base forecaster ETS.
- **Baselines:** ACI and EnbPI (`ConformalForecaster`); fixed split-conformal intervals.
- **Expected metric:** long-run coverage, local coverage over windows of 50, and mean width. Hypothesis: all online methods reach 90% long-run coverage; PID has the smallest local-coverage deviation after a shift and narrower intervals than ACI on trending series; fixed split-conformal under-covers after the variance burst.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2307.16895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16895)

### 12.2.11 Early-exit classifiers: BranchyNet and shallow-deep networks, judged by failure prediction (Teerapittayanon et al., ICPR 2016; Kaya, Hong & Dumitras, ICML 2019; Kubaty et al., NeurIPS 2025)
Early-exit networks attach internal classifiers after intermediate blocks and stop at the first whose confidence passes a threshold, saving compute on easy inputs. - **Expected metric:** accuracy vs average MACs curve, overthinking rate, and the correlation of ECE and EEFP with the area under the accuracy–compute curve. ### 12.2.13 DP-SGD clipping follow-ups with reusable per-example clipping: adaptive quantile clipping, automatic clipping and augmentation multiplicity (Andrew et al., NeurIPS 2021; Bu et al., NeurIPS 2023; De et al., 2022)
DP-SGD's clip norm C is its most sensitive hyperparameter. Adaptive clipping tracks a target quantile of per-example gradient norms with a private geometric update; automatic clipping normalises every per-example gradient (g / (‖g‖ + γ)), removing C; augmentation multiplicity averages each example's gradient over K augmentations before clipping, which reduces noise without extra privacy cost. The enabling change exposes `DpSgd.clipPerExample` (and the noise step) as public static methods so that wave 11's `DpFtrl` copy and these variants share one implementation; `DpSgd`'s behaviour is unchanged.
- **Block:** `DpSgd.clipPerExample`/`addNoise` (made public); `experimental.privacy.ClippingStrategies` (ADAPTIVE_QUANTILE, AUTOMATIC, AUG_MULTIPLICITY) using the RDP accountant unchanged.
- **Experiment:** `FederatedFollowUps` (DP table) or a new method in `experiments.safety.PrivateTraining` (owned by WP2 in this wave).
- **Toy task:** digits MLP at ε ∈ {1, 3, 8}, δ = 1e-5, 3 epochs; augmentation with core `Augmentation` operations (digit-safe translations; public operations if landed).
- **Baselines:** `DpSgd` with C swept over {0.1, 0.5, 1, 5} (best C chosen non-privately, as an optimistic baseline); wave 11 `DpFtrl`.
- **Expected metric:** test accuracy at each ε and sensitivity to the initial C. Hypothesis: adaptive and automatic clipping reach within 1 point of the best fixed C without tuning; augmentation multiplicity (K = 4) adds 1–3 points at ε = 3.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1905.03871](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03871), [arXiv:2206.07136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.07136), [arXiv:2204.13650](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.13650)

### 12.2.14 Lossless compression with learned models: arithmetic coding and rANS driven by n-gram and neural LMs (Witten, Neal & Cleary, CACM 1987; Duda, 2013; Delétang et al., ICLR 2024)
Any probabilistic model is a compressor: arithmetic coding (or the faster rANS) spends −log₂ p(x_t | x_<t) bits per symbol, so the code length equals the model's log loss plus a few bits. "Language modeling is compression" shows that the accounting must include the model size, and that small domain models beat general compressors on their own data. Here the repo's LMs become codecs, and the tests check exact round-trips.
- **Block:** `experimental.codec.ArithmeticCoder` (32-bit range arithmetic coder), `experimental.codec.Rans` (static and adaptive), `experimental.codec.ModelCodec` (wraps any next-symbol distribution: adaptive order-k context mixing, `ByteNgramModel`, `KneserNeyLm`, `WindowLm`).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.coding.EntropyCoding`.
- **Toy task:** `SyntheticCorpus` text, `KeyStreams` symbol streams, quantised weight tensors from a trained MLP, 10–200 kB each.
- **Baselines:** `HuffmanCoder` (order-0); `java.util.zip.Deflater` at level 9; empirical order-0 entropy.
- **Expected metric:** bits per symbol including (and excluding) model size, encode/decode throughput, and exact round-trip. Hypothesis: adaptive order-3 context models beat Deflate by 20–40% on synthetic text; the Kneser–Ney and window LMs beat both excluding model size, and lose to them including model size on small files; arithmetic coding is within 0.01 bits/symbol of the model's log loss.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2309.10668](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10668); Duda, "Asymmetric numeral systems" ([arXiv:1311.2540](https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.2540)).

### 12.2.15 Learned image compression: factorised prior and scale hyperprior autoencoders (Ballé, Laparra & Simoncelli, ICLR 2017; Ballé et al., ICLR 2018)
End-to-end image codecs train an encoder, a quantiser and a decoder for rate + λ·distortion. During training, quantisation is replaced by additive uniform noise and the rate is the negative log-likelihood of the latents under a learned factorised density; the hyperprior model sends side information z that predicts each latent's scale, which captures spatial dependence. At test time the latents are rounded and coded with 12.2.14's arithmetic coder, so the reported bits are real.
- **Block:** `experimental.codec.LearnedImageCodec` (FACTORIZED, HYPERPRIOR; GDN-lite or ReLU convolution stacks; λ sweep), using the 12.2.14 coder.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.coding.LearnedCompression`.
- **Toy task:** 16×16 and 28×28 `ShapeImages` and digits, 4–6 λ values.
- **Baselines:** a JPEG-like codec (8×8 DCT, quantisation table scaled by quality, zig-zag run-length plus the 12.2.14 coder); PCA with quantised coefficients; VQ-VAE codes from `ImageAutoencoders` coded at log₂(K) bits.
- **Expected metric:** rate–distortion curves (bits per pixel vs PSNR) and BD-rate relative to the DCT codec. Hypothesis: the factorised model beats the DCT codec at low rates on these synthetic shapes, the hyperprior saves a further 5–15% rate; VQ codes are competitive only at one rate.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1611.01704](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.01704), [arXiv:1802.01436](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01436)

### 12.2.16 Bits-back coding with latent variable models: BB-ANS (Townsend, Bird & Barber, ICLR 2019)
A VAE gives an ELBO, not a likelihood, so coding x needs a latent z. Bits-back coding first decodes z from the bits already in the ANS stack using q(z | x) (getting those bits "back"), then encodes x under p(x | z) and z under p(z); the net code length is the negative ELBO. Chaining images amortises the initial bits. The method needs the stack-like rANS of 12.2.14 and discretised latents.
- **Block:** `experimental.codec.BitsBackCoder` (BB-ANS over a small binarised-digit VAE with discretised Gaussian latents).
- **Experiment:** `EntropyCoding` (latent-model table).
- **Toy task:** binarised digits (8×8 and 28×28), chains of 100–1000 images.
- **Baselines:** 12.2.14's coder with a per-pixel independent Bernoulli model; Deflate; the VAE's ELBO in bits (the target).
- **Expected metric:** bits per image vs the negative ELBO, and the overhead of the first image. Hypothesis: BB-ANS reaches within 1–2% of the negative ELBO over a chain of 1000 images, beating the independent model by 30–50% and Deflate by more; the first image costs extra initial bits that are amortised after about 20 images.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1901.04866](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.04866)

### 12.3.1 Histograms of oriented gradients with a linear SVM, by sliding window and hard-negative mining (Dalal & Triggs, CVPR 2005)
HOG computes gradient orientations per pixel, bins them by magnitude into cells, normalises blocks of cells (L2-Hys), and concatenates the blocks into a descriptor that is robust to illumination and small deformations. A linear SVM over windows, retrained with hard negatives mined from false positives, gives the classic pedestrian detector; non-maximum suppression merges overlapping windows.
- **Block:** `experimental.vision.HogDescriptor` and `experimental.vision.SlidingWindowDetector` (multi-scale pyramid, hard-negative mining, NMS from `DetectionMetrics`/`Boxes`), using `KernelSvm` in linear mode or core linear SGD.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.vision.ClassicalDetection`.
- **Toy task:** `ShapeScenes` single-class detection (one shape type among distractors), 64×64 scenes; training-window augmentation with core `Augmentation` operations (public ones if landed).
- **Baselines:** `CenterNetHead` and `AnchorDetector` (wave 8) at equal training time; raw-pixel windows with the same SVM.
- **Expected metric:** AP@0.5, false positives per image and CPU time per image. Hypothesis: HOG+SVM beats raw pixels by 20+ AP points and matches the CNN detectors at small training sets (≤ 200 images), with lower AP at larger sets; hard-negative mining adds 5–10 AP.
- **Source:** Dalal & Triggs, "Histograms of oriented gradients for human detection" (CVPR 2005).

### 12.3.2 Viola–Jones: integral images, Haar-like features and an AdaBoost attentional cascade (Viola & Jones, CVPR 2001)
An integral image makes any rectangle sum four lookups, so thousands of Haar-like two-, three- and four-rectangle features are cheap; AdaBoost selects a few as decision stumps; a cascade of increasingly strong boosted stages, each tuned for a very high detection rate, rejects most windows after a handful of features, which is what makes detection real-time.
- **Block:** `experimental.vision.HaarCascade` (integral image, feature enumeration with sub-sampling, AdaBoost stumps, cascade training with per-stage targets).
- **Experiment:** `ClassicalDetection`.
- **Toy task:** as 12.3.1.
- **Baselines:** 12.3.1's HOG+SVM; the CNN detectors; a single (non-cascaded) boosted classifier with the same features.
- **Expected metric:** AP@0.5, average features evaluated per window, and time per image. Hypothesis: the cascade evaluates under 10 features per window on average and is the fastest detector by 5–20×, at a few AP points below HOG+SVM; the non-cascaded booster matches its accuracy at 10× the cost.
- **Source:** Viola & Jones, "Rapid object detection using a boosted cascade of simple features" (CVPR 2001).

### 12.3.3 Superpixels and graph-based segmentation: SLIC, Felzenszwalb–Huttenlocher and the watershed (Achanta et al., TPAMI 2012; Felzenszwalb & Huttenlocher, IJCV 2004; Vincent & Soille, TPAMI 1991)
SLIC runs a localised k-means in (colour, x, y) space from a grid of seeds, giving compact superpixels; the Felzenszwalb–Huttenlocher algorithm merges pixels along minimum-spanning-tree edges while the edge weight is below each component's internal difference plus k/|C|; the watershed floods a gradient image from markers. Superpixels are also a cheap input for a downstream labeller (per-superpixel features + a classifier).
- **Block:** `experimental.vision.Superpixels` (SLIC, FELZENSZWALB, WATERSHED) and a superpixel-pooling helper.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.vision.ClassicalSegmentation`.
- **Toy task:** `ShapeScenes` with textured backgrounds and `DegradedImages` noise, ground-truth instance masks.
- **Baselines:** k-means on pixel colours; mean shift (`EmbeddingClustering`); the U-Net (`UNet2d`) for the downstream labelling task.
- **Expected metric:** boundary recall, under-segmentation error and achievable segmentation accuracy at 50–400 superpixels; downstream IoU with a per-superpixel MLP vs the U-Net. Hypothesis: SLIC and FH reach boundary recall > 0.9 at 200 segments where k-means on colour is below 0.7; the superpixel classifier gets within 5 IoU points of the U-Net at 10× less training.
- **Source:** Achanta et al., "SLIC superpixels compared to state-of-the-art superpixel methods" (TPAMI 2012); Felzenszwalb & Huttenlocher (IJCV 2004).

### 12.3.4 Graph cuts for MRF segmentation and denoising: max-flow/min-cut, α-expansion, ICM and loopy BP (Greig, Porteous & Seheult, JRSS-B 1989; Boykov & Kolmogorov, TPAMI 2004; Boykov, Veksler & Zabih, TPAMI 2001; Besag, JRSS-B 1986)
A binary MRF with submodular pairwise terms (unary data costs + Potts smoothing) is solved exactly by a minimum s–t cut; Boykov–Kolmogorov's augmenting-path algorithm with search-tree reuse is fast on grids. Multi-label Potts energies are minimised approximately by α-expansion (a sequence of binary cuts, within a factor 2 of the optimum). ICM (greedy coordinate updates) and loopy belief propagation are the weaker classics; GrabCut-style iterations alternate GMM colour models and cuts.
- **Block:** `experimental.vision.GraphCuts` (BK max-flow, BINARY_MRF, ALPHA_EXPANSION, ICM, GRABCUT_LITE with `GaussianMixture`); loopy BP reuses `FactorGraph` if its grid interface suffices, else a local grid BP.
- **Experiment:** `ClassicalSegmentation` (MRF table).
- **Toy task:** binary shape masks with salt-and-pepper and Gaussian noise; 3–4-label colour segmentation of `ShapeScenes`; interactive foreground extraction from a box.
- **Baselines:** per-pixel thresholding/argmax of the unaries; median filtering; `UNet2d` trained on the same noise.
- **Expected metric:** pixel error and energy reached, and time per image. Hypothesis: graph cuts reach the exact minimum energy and halve the pixel error of thresholding at high noise; α-expansion reaches lower energy than ICM and BP; a U-Net beats the MRF only when trained on the exact noise model.
- **Source:** Boykov & Kolmogorov, "An experimental comparison of min-cut/max-flow algorithms for energy minimization in vision" (TPAMI 2004).

### 12.3.5 Hough transforms for lines and circles, against RANSAC (Duda & Hart, CACM 1972; Ballard, Pattern Recognition 1981; Fischler & Bolles, CACM 1981)
The Hough transform lets every edge point vote for all lines (ρ, θ) through it, so peaks in the accumulator are lines even with heavy clutter and occlusion; circles vote in (x, y, r) or use gradient direction to cut the voting; the generalised Hough transform votes for arbitrary template positions. RANSAC instead samples minimal sets and counts inliers, one model at a time.
- **Block:** `experimental.vision.HoughTransform` (LINES with non-maximum suppression, CIRCLES with gradient voting, PROBABILISTIC sampling variant, GENERALISED for a template).
- **Experiment:** `ClassicalSegmentation` (geometry-primitive table).
- **Toy task:** synthetic edge maps with 1–6 lines and circles, 10–60% clutter points and gaps; edges from wave 11 `EdgeDetectors` (Canny) on `ShapeScenes`.
- **Baselines:** sequential RANSAC from wave 11 `FeatureMatching` (read-only, or a local copy of its loop if private); least-squares fits.
- **Expected metric:** detection F1 and parameter error vs clutter and number of primitives. Hypothesis: RANSAC wins for a single primitive with little clutter; Hough is better with 4+ primitives and 40%+ clutter; least squares fails beyond 10% clutter.
- **Source:** Duda & Hart, "Use of the Hough transformation to detect lines and curves in pictures" (CACM 1972).

### 12.3.6 Two-view geometry: the normalised eight-point algorithm, essential matrices and triangulation (Longuet-Higgins, Nature 1981; Hartley, TPAMI 1997; Nistér, TPAMI 2004; Hartley & Sturm, CVIU 1997)
The fundamental matrix F satisfies x'ᵀFx = 0 for corresponding points; the eight-point algorithm solves it linearly by SVD, but only after Hartley's normalisation (centre and scale the points) is it accurate. With known intrinsics, the essential matrix E = [t]×R decomposes into four (R, t) candidates, of which the cheirality check keeps one; linear (DLT) or midpoint triangulation recovers 3-D points, refined by minimising reprojection error. RANSAC over the eight-point (or seven-point) solver handles outliers.
- **Block:** `experimental.geometry.TwoViewGeometry` (EIGHT_POINT raw and normalised, essential decomposition with cheirality, DLT triangulation, Sampson error, RANSAC wrapper).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.robotics.MultiViewGeometry`.
- **Toy task:** `GeometryScenes` two-view pairs, 50–200 correspondences, 0.5–2 px noise, 0–50% outliers, near-planar and general scenes.
- **Baselines:** the unnormalised eight-point algorithm; the homography + RANSAC of wave 11 `FeatureMatching` (the wrong model for general scenes).
- **Expected metric:** rotation and translation-direction error, Sampson error and triangulation error. Hypothesis: normalisation cuts the pose error 5–10× at 1 px noise; RANSAC keeps errors flat to 50% outliers; the homography model fails on non-planar scenes.
- **Source:** Hartley, "In defense of the eight-point algorithm" (TPAMI 1997).

### 12.3.7 Camera pose and structure refinement: EPnP and bundle adjustment with a Schur complement (Lepetit, Moreno-Noguer & Fua, IJCV 2009; Triggs et al., 2000)
PnP estimates a camera pose from n 3-D–2-D correspondences; EPnP writes the points as weighted sums of four control points and solves in O(n). Bundle adjustment jointly refines all camera poses and 3-D points by Levenberg–Marquardt on reprojection error; the normal equations have a block-sparse structure, and eliminating the points by a Schur complement leaves a small camera system. Robust (Huber) losses down-weight outliers.
- **Block:** `experimental.geometry.PoseEstimation` (DLT, EPNP, Gauss–Newton refinement) and `experimental.geometry.BundleAdjustment` (LM with Schur complement, Huber loss, gauge fixing), using `LevenbergMarquardt`'s damping logic unchanged where it fits.
- **Experiment:** `MultiViewGeometry`.
- **Toy task:** `GeometryScenes` sequences of 5–20 cameras around 100–300 points, initialised from 12.3.6 with noise and drift.
- **Baselines:** DLT pose; the uncompressed (dense) LM solve; no refinement.
- **Expected metric:** reprojection RMSE, pose and point errors, and time per iteration. Hypothesis: EPnP beats DLT at n ≤ 10; BA reduces reprojection error to the noise floor in under 20 iterations, and the Schur complement is 10× faster than the dense solve at 20 cameras with identical results.
- **Source:** Lepetit, Moreno-Noguer & Fua, "EPnP: an accurate O(n) solution to the PnP problem" (IJCV 2009); Triggs et al., "Bundle adjustment — a modern synthesis" (2000).

### 12.3.8 Point-cloud registration: ICP (point-to-point and point-to-plane) and Deep Closest Point (Besl & McKay, TPAMI 1992; Chen & Medioni, 1992; Wang & Solomon, ICCV 2019)
ICP alternates nearest-neighbour correspondences with the closed-form (SVD/Kabsch) rigid alignment; point-to-plane ICP linearises the rotation and converges faster on smooth surfaces; both need a good initial pose. Deep Closest Point embeds points with a DGCNN and a transformer, forms soft correspondences by attention, and solves the alignment with a differentiable SVD, so it works from large initial rotations.
- **Block:** `experimental.geometry.PointRegistration` (ICP_POINT, ICP_PLANE with normal estimation, trimmed ICP) and `experimental.geometry.DeepClosestPoint` (reusing `EdgeConv` from wave 8 and core attention).
- **Experiment:** `MultiViewGeometry` (registration table).
- **Toy task:** `GeometryScenes` partial point clouds (64–512 points) of shapes with rotations up to 90°, noise and partial overlap.
- **Baselines:** Kabsch with known correspondences (`TransportGeometry`, the oracle); a `PointNet` regressor of the pose.
- **Expected metric:** rotation and translation error vs initial angle, and success rate (error < 5°). Hypothesis: ICP is exact below about 30° and fails beyond 60°; DCP succeeds at 90° but is less precise, and DCP followed by ICP is best; the PointNet regressor is worst.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1905.03304](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03304)

### 12.3.9 Simultaneous localisation and mapping: EKF-SLAM, FastSLAM 2.0 and pose-graph optimisation (Smith, Self & Cheeseman, 1990; Montemerlo et al., AAAI 2002 and IJCAI 2003; Lu & Milios, 1997; Grisetti et al., 2010)
EKF-SLAM keeps a joint Gaussian over the robot pose and all landmarks, with a quadratic-cost covariance update; FastSLAM factors the posterior into a particle filter over paths and one small EKF per landmark per particle, and version 2.0 samples the pose from a proposal that includes the measurement. Graph-based SLAM instead keeps all poses as nodes with odometry and loop-closure constraints and solves the nonlinear least squares by Gauss–Newton on the sparse information matrix.
- **Block:** `experimental.geometry.Slam` (EKF_SLAM, FASTSLAM2, POSE_GRAPH with Gauss–Newton and SE(2) residuals), with known data association and a nearest-neighbour association option.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.robotics.MappingAndLocalisation`.
- **Toy task:** `GeometryScenes` 2-D worlds with 20–100 landmarks, range-bearing sensing, noisy odometry, loops of 200–1000 steps.
- **Baselines:** dead reckoning; EKF localisation with a known map (the `TrackingFilters` EKF, read-only, as the oracle); a particle-filter localiser with a known map.
- **Expected metric:** absolute trajectory error, landmark RMSE and time per step. Hypothesis: all SLAM methods bound drift where dead reckoning grows linearly; pose-graph optimisation has the lowest error after loop closure; FastSLAM 2.0 with 20 particles matches EKF-SLAM and scales better with landmarks.
- **Source:** Montemerlo et al., "FastSLAM 2.0" (IJCAI 2003); Grisetti et al., "A tutorial on graph-based SLAM" (2010).

### 12.3.10 Occupancy grid mapping with log-odds updates and a learned inverse sensor model (Elfes, Computer 1989; Moravec, 1988; Thrun, Autonomous Robots 2003)
An occupancy grid keeps the log-odds of each cell being occupied and adds an inverse-sensor-model increment for each range beam (free along the ray, occupied at the hit); clamping keeps the map responsive to change. A learned inverse sensor model (a small CNN over a local scan) can handle noisy or specular sensors better than the hand-made cone model.
- **Block:** `experimental.geometry.OccupancyGrid` (log-odds, ray casting with Bresenham, CONE and LEARNED inverse models, clamping).
- **Experiment:** `MappingAndLocalisation` (mapping table).
- **Toy task:** `PlanningMaps` rooms scanned by a simulated lidar from a known trajectory, with beam noise, max-range readings and 5% spurious returns; a moved-obstacle scenario.
- **Baselines:** hit counting (cells marked occupied by any hit); the cone model without clamping.
- **Expected metric:** map AUROC/accuracy against the true grid and recovery time after an obstacle moves. Hypothesis: log-odds maps reach > 0.95 accuracy where hit counting suffers from spurious returns; clamping recovers moved obstacles 5× faster; the learned model helps only under the specular noise model.
- **Source:** Elfes, "Using occupancy grids for mobile robot perception and navigation" (Computer 1989).

### 12.3.11 Graph search for planning: Dijkstra, A*, weighted A* and jump point search (Dijkstra, 1959; Hart, Nilsson & Raphael, 1968; Pohl, 1970; Harabor & Grastien, AAAI 2011)
A* expands nodes by f = g + h; with an admissible, consistent heuristic it is optimal and expands no more nodes than any other optimal algorithm with that heuristic; weighted A* (f = g + w·h) trades optimality (within a factor w) for speed; jump point search prunes symmetric paths on uniform-cost grids by jumping along straight lines to forced neighbours, often 10× fewer expansions with optimality kept.
- **Block:** `experimental.planning.GraphSearch` (BFS, DIJKSTRA, ASTAR, WEIGHTED_ASTAR, JPS; Manhattan, octile and Euclidean heuristics; tie-breaking options).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.robotics.SearchPlanning`.
- **Toy task:** `PlanningMaps` grids (random obstacles 10–40%, mazes, rooms) at 64² and 128², 4- and 8-connected.
- **Baselines:** BFS; greedy best-first search.
- **Expected metric:** nodes expanded, path cost ratio to optimal, and time. Hypothesis: A* expands 3–10× fewer nodes than Dijkstra; JPS cuts expansions another 5–20× on open maps and less in mazes, with identical costs; weighted A* (w = 2) halves A* expansions at < 5% cost overhead.
- **Source:** Harabor & Grastien, "Online graph pruning for pathfinding on grid maps" (AAAI 2011).

### 12.3.12 Learned heuristics for search: DeepCubeA-style approximate value iteration and empirically admissible neural heuristics (Agostinelli et al., Nature Machine Intelligence 2019; Sahay, 2026)
DeepCubeA trains a cost-to-go network by approximate value iteration on states scrambled backwards from the goal (no human data), then runs batch weighted A* with it. The 2026 paper trains with an underestimating Bellman update and an asymmetric loss that penalises overestimates, then subtracts a safety offset measured on validation scrambles, giving a heuristic that is admissible on the validation distribution and keeps A*'s optimality empirically.
- **Block:** `experimental.planning.LearnedHeuristic` (DAVI with an MLP, ASYMMETRIC_UNDERESTIMATE with offset calibration), used by 12.3.11's A*.
- **Experiment:** `SearchPlanning` (puzzle table).
- **Toy task:** `PlanningMaps` 8-puzzle and 3×3/4×4 Lights Out (optimal costs by BFS), and 15-puzzle instances of scramble depth ≤ 30.
- **Baselines:** A* with Manhattan distance (+ linear conflict); BFS; a plain MSE-trained cost-to-go network.
- **Expected metric:** solution optimality rate, nodes expanded and inadmissibility rate. Hypothesis: the MSE network overestimates on 10–30% of states and returns suboptimal paths; the asymmetric, offset-calibrated network is optimal on ≥ 99% of test instances while expanding 2–5× fewer nodes than Manhattan; DAVI with weighted A* expands the fewest nodes at a small optimality loss.
- **Source:** Agostinelli, McAleer, Shmakov & Baldi, "Solving the Rubik's cube with deep reinforcement learning and search" (Nature Machine Intelligence 2019; precursor [arXiv:1805.07470](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07470)), [arXiv:2606.04860](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.04860)

### 12.3.13 Differentiable planners: value iteration networks and Neural A* (Tamar et al., NeurIPS 2016; Yonetani et al., ICML 2021)
A value iteration network embeds K steps of value iteration as a recurrent convolution with a max over action channels, so a policy trained by imitation learns a planning computation that generalises to new maps. Neural A* makes A* differentiable (a soft node selection and matrix-form expansion) and trains a CNN to predict a guidance cost map from the input image so that the differentiable search expands few nodes while matching expert paths.
- **Block:** `experimental.planning.ValueIterationNetwork` and `experimental.planning.NeuralAStar` (differentiable A* with straight-through argmin, a small U-Net-style encoder).
- **Experiment:** `SearchPlanning` (learned-planner table).
- **Toy task:** `PlanningMaps` 32² grids with random obstacles and rooms; train on 500–2,000 maps with A* expert paths, test on unseen maps and larger 64² maps.
- **Baselines:** 12.3.11's A* (optimal, the expansions reference); a CNN that predicts the next action directly; `GridWorld`-style tabular value iteration (oracle).
- **Expected metric:** success rate, path-cost ratio, and nodes expanded (Neural A*). Hypothesis: VIN beats the reactive CNN in success on unseen maps by 20+ points and degrades on 64² maps unless K grows; Neural A* expands 30–50% fewer nodes than A* at < 5% path-cost overhead.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1602.02867](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.02867), [arXiv:2009.07476](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.07476)

### 12.3.14 Sampling-based motion planning: PRM, RRT, RRT* and learned sampling distributions (Kavraki et al., IEEE T-RA 1996; LaValle, 1998; Karaman & Frazzoli, IJRR 2011; Ichter, Harrison & Pavone, ICRA 2018)
Probabilistic roadmaps sample the free configuration space, connect nearby samples, and answer queries by graph search; RRT grows a tree towards random samples and finds feasible paths quickly in high dimensions; RRT* rewires the tree within a shrinking radius and is asymptotically optimal. Learned samplers train a conditional generative model (a CVAE) on samples from previous optimal paths so that RRT/PRM samples concentrate where solutions lie.
- **Block:** `experimental.planning.SamplingPlanners` (PRM, RRT, RRT_CONNECT, RRT_STAR, informed sampling) and `experimental.planning.LearnedSampler` (a CVAE on obstacle encodings).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.robotics.MotionPlanning`.
- **Toy task:** `PlanningMaps` continuous 2-D obstacle fields with narrow passages, a 2-D point robot and a 3-link planar arm (3-D configuration space).
- **Baselines:** 12.3.11's A* on a discretised grid (resolution-complete); uniform sampling.
- **Expected metric:** success within N samples, path cost over time, and collision checks. Hypothesis: RRT-Connect finds paths fastest, RRT* approaches the grid-A* cost as samples grow; the learned sampler cuts samples to first solution 2–5× in narrow passages; the grid planner wins in 2-D and loses for the 3-link arm.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1105.1186](https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1186), [arXiv:1709.05448](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.05448)

### 12.3.15 Inverse kinematics: Jacobian pseudo-inverse, damped least squares, CCD, FABRIK and learned multi-solution IK (Wampler, 1986; Nakamura & Hanafusa, 1986; Aristidou & Lasenby, Graphical Models 2011; Ames et al., ICRA 2022)
Numerical IK iterates joint updates from the end-effector error: the Jacobian pseudo-inverse is fast but unstable near singularities, damped least squares (Levenberg–Marquardt) trades accuracy for stability; cyclic coordinate descent rotates one joint at a time; FABRIK moves joint positions forward and backward along the chain with no matrices. Redundant arms have many solutions, and IKFlow learns a normalising flow over them conditioned on the target pose, giving diverse solutions in one pass.
- **Block:** `experimental.planning.InverseKinematics` (PSEUDO_INVERSE, DAMPED_LEAST_SQUARES, CCD, FABRIK, joint limits) and `experimental.planning.LearnedIk` (a conditional affine-coupling flow; MDN option).
- **Experiment:** `MotionPlanning` (IK table).
- **Toy task:** planar 2-, 3- and 7-link arms with joint limits; reachable targets near singularities; multi-solution coverage.
- **Baselines:** `MultipleHypotheses`' two-link WTA arm (wave 11, read-only); a direct MLP regressor (which averages solutions).
- **Expected metric:** position error, success rate, iterations, and solution diversity (for learned IK). Hypothesis: DLS and FABRIK succeed on > 99% of targets where the pseudo-inverse diverges near singularities; the MLP regressor fails on redundant arms; the flow covers several solution branches with errors that one DLS refinement step removes.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2111.08933](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.08933); Aristidou & Lasenby, "FABRIK" (Graphical Models 2011).

### 12.3.16 Safety filters: control barrier functions over a learned policy (Ames et al., ECC 2019; Cheng et al., AAAI 2019; Dawson, Gao & Fan, T-RO 2023)
A control barrier function h(x) ≥ 0 defines a safe set; any controller can be filtered by a small quadratic program that changes the action as little as possible subject to ḣ + α(h) ≥ 0, which keeps the state safe for control-affine dynamics. Wrapping an RL policy in a CBF filter gives safety during exploration (Cheng et al.); learned neural CBFs fit h from data when an analytic one is unknown. The filter wraps the step-by-step SAC/TD3 drivers of 12.6.17 (wave 11's `StepwiseTd3` until those land).
- **Block:** `experimental.planning.ControlBarrierFilter` (analytic CBF with a closed-form or active-set QP for one or two constraints, LEARNED_CBF with a hinge loss on safe/unsafe samples).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.robotics.SafeControl`.
- **Toy task:** a double-integrator point robot reaching a goal past circular obstacles; Pendulum with an angle constraint (the `CostedEnvironment` style cost signal for the baselines).
- **Baselines:** `ConstrainedPolicyOptimization` (CPO and PPO-Lagrangian); unfiltered SAC/TD3 (`StepwiseTd3`); a reward-penalty SAC.
- **Expected metric:** constraint violations during training and at test, and return. Hypothesis: the analytic CBF filter gives zero violations from the first episode with a small return loss; the Lagrangian methods violate often early and converge to few violations; the learned CBF reduces violations by 90% but not to zero.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1903.11199](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.11199), [arXiv:1903.08792](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08792)

### 12.3.17 System identification: ARX/ARMAX least squares, output-error models and N4SID subspace identification (Ljung, 1999; Van Overschee & De Moor, Automatica 1994)
Before learning dynamics with neural networks, control engineers fit linear models: ARX regresses the output on past outputs and inputs by least squares (biased under coloured noise); ARMAX and output-error models fix that with pseudo-linear regression or prediction-error minimisation; N4SID projects future outputs on past data with oblique projections and reads the state-space order and (A, B, C, D) from an SVD, with no iterative optimisation.
- **Block:** `experimental.geometry.SystemIdentification` (ARX by OLS — core `LeastSquares.ols` if landed —, ARMAX by pseudo-linear regression, OUTPUT_ERROR by Gauss–Newton, N4SID with SVD order selection).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.robotics.SystemIdentification`.
- **Toy task:** linearised `MechanicalSystems` (mass–spring–damper chains, a DC motor), 2nd–6th order, white and coloured noise, PRBS and multisine excitation; a mildly nonlinear system for the limits.
- **Baselines:** `Sindy`; DMD from wave 8 (`DynamicsLearning`); wave 11 `LinearDynamicalSystemEm`.
- **Expected metric:** simulation (free-run) fit percentage, one-step error, and pole error. Hypothesis: N4SID recovers order and poles to < 2% error from 1,000 samples and matches LDS EM at 10× less time; ARX is biased under coloured noise where ARMAX/OE are not; SINDy wins only on the nonlinear system.
- **Source:** Van Overschee & De Moor, "N4SID: subspace algorithms for the identification of combined deterministic-stochastic systems" (Automatica 1994).

### 12.4.1 Pairwise sequence alignment: Needleman–Wunsch, Smith–Waterman with affine gaps, and a differentiable Smith–Waterman with learned scores (Needleman & Wunsch, JMB 1970; Smith & Waterman, JMB 1981; Gotoh, JMB 1982; Petti et al., Bioinformatics 2023)
Global (NW) and local (SW) alignment are dynamic programs over a substitution matrix and gap penalties; Gotoh's three-matrix recursion handles affine gaps in O(nm). Replacing max by a temperature-scaled log-sum-exp gives a smooth SW whose gradient is the expected alignment, so substitution scores (or embeddings that produce them) can be learned end to end from homologous pairs, as in SMURF's learned alignment.
- **Block:** `experimental.bio.SequenceAlignment` (NW, SW, affine gaps, traceback, SOFT_SW with gradients) and `experimental.bio.LearnedScoring` (a learned substitution matrix or a 1-D CNN embedding per residue).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.biology.SequenceAnalysis`.
- **Toy task:** `BioSequences` homologous pairs at 10–70% divergence with indels, true alignments kept; homology search among 500 decoys.
- **Baselines:** edit distance (the OCR/ASR metric code); k-mer Jaccard via `MinHashDeduplicator`; SW with a fixed identity matrix.
- **Expected metric:** alignment accuracy (fraction of correctly aligned columns) and homology-search ROC AUC vs divergence. Hypothesis: affine-gap SW beats edit distance at high divergence; the learned scores recover the generating substitution model and raise alignment accuracy by 5–15 points at 60% divergence; k-mer methods collapse beyond 40% divergence.
- **Source:** Gotoh, "An improved algorithm for matching biological sequences" (JMB 1982); Petti et al., "End-to-end learning of multiple sequence alignments with differentiable Smith–Waterman" (Bioinformatics 2023).

### 12.4.2 Profile hidden Markov models for family detection (Krogh et al., JMB 1994; Eddy, Bioinformatics 1998)
A profile HMM has match, insert and delete states per alignment column; it is built from a multiple alignment (with pseudocounts) or trained by Baum–Welch from unaligned family members, and scores new sequences by the forward algorithm (log-odds against a null model). It captures position-specific conservation that pairwise scores miss.
- **Block:** `experimental.bio.ProfileHmm` (construction from an alignment, Baum–Welch training, forward/Viterbi scoring, null-model log-odds).
- **Experiment:** `SequenceAnalysis` (family-detection table).
- **Toy task:** `BioSequences` families of 10–50 members at 30–60% identity, sequences from 20 families plus random decoys.
- **Baselines:** best SW score against family members (12.4.1); `HiddenMarkovModel` with a flat topology; a k-mer logistic regression; a 1-D CNN classifier.
- **Expected metric:** family-detection AUROC and accuracy with 5–50 training members. Hypothesis: profile HMMs beat best-pairwise SW by 0.05–0.15 AUROC at 30% identity; the CNN needs 50+ members to match them.
- **Source:** Krogh et al., "Hidden Markov models in computational biology" (JMB 1994); Eddy, "Profile hidden Markov models" (Bioinformatics 1998).

### 12.4.3 Motif discovery: MEME, the Gibbs motif sampler and DeepBind-style CNN filters (Bailey & Elkan, ISMB 1994; Lawrence et al., Science 1993; Alipanahi et al., Nature Biotechnology 2015)
MEME fits a two-component mixture (motif PWM vs background) by EM, with zero-or-one occurrence per sequence; the Gibbs sampler resamples one sequence's motif site at a time given the PWM of the others; DeepBind trains a convolutional network to classify bound vs unbound sequences, and its first-layer filters, read as PWMs, recover the motif.
- **Block:** `experimental.bio.MotifFinder` (MEME_EM with ZOOPS, GIBBS_SAMPLER) and `experimental.bio.MotifCnn` (one-hot 1-D CNN with max-pool, filter-to-PWM conversion).
- **Experiment:** `SequenceAnalysis` (motif table).
- **Toy task:** `BioSequences` 200–1000 sequences of length 100–200 with a planted 8–12-mer PWM in 30–100% of positives, and decoys.
- **Baselines:** k-mer enrichment (counting over-represented 8-mers); a k-mer logistic regression classifier.
- **Expected metric:** motif recovery (KL divergence / Pearson correlation of PWM columns with the truth), site-level F1, and bound/unbound AUROC. Hypothesis: MEME and Gibbs recover the PWM when it occurs in ≥ 50% of sequences; DeepBind gives the best classification AUROC and recovers a usable PWM from ≥ 500 sequences; k-mer enrichment finds the consensus but not the PWM.
- **Source:** Bailey & Elkan (ISMB 1994); Alipanahi et al., "Predicting the sequence specificities of DNA- and RNA-binding proteins by deep learning" (Nature Biotechnology 2015).

### 12.4.4 Phylogenetic inference: UPGMA, neighbour joining, maximum likelihood with Felsenstein pruning, and PhyloGFN (Sokal & Michener, 1958; Saitou & Nei, MBE 1987; Felsenstein, J. Mol. Evol. 1981; Zhou et al., ICLR 2024)
Distance methods build trees from pairwise evolutionary distances: UPGMA assumes a molecular clock, neighbour joining does not and is consistent for additive distances. Maximum likelihood scores a tree by Felsenstein's pruning algorithm under a substitution model and searches by nearest-neighbour interchange. PhyloGFN trains a GFlowNet that builds trees by merging subtrees, with reward exp(−parsimony/T) or the likelihood, so it samples a diverse set of high-scoring trees.
- **Block:** `experimental.bio.Phylogenetics` (JC/K2P distances, UPGMA, NJ, Fitch parsimony, Felsenstein likelihood, NNI search) and `experimental.bio.PhyloGfn` (reusing `GFlowNet`'s trajectory balance unchanged).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.biology.StructureAndPhylogeny`.
- **Toy task:** `BioSequences` alignments of 8–20 taxa, 200–1000 sites, clock-like and strongly non-clock trees.
- **Baselines:** the true tree (for scoring); random trees; UPGMA as the naive baseline.
- **Expected metric:** Robinson–Foulds distance to the true tree, log-likelihood, and (for PhyloGFN) number of distinct near-optimal trees sampled. Hypothesis: NJ beats UPGMA on non-clock trees; ML+NNI is best at 1,000 sites; PhyloGFN finds parsimony-optimal trees on 8–12 taxa and returns several distinct near-optimal topologies.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2310.08774](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08774); Saitou & Nei, "The neighbor-joining method" (MBE 1987).

### 12.4.5 Coevolution and contact prediction: Potts models by pseudo-likelihood with the average product correction (Weigt et al., PNAS 2009; Ekeberg et al., PRE 2013; Dunn, Wahl & Gloor, Bioinformatics 2008)
Residues in contact co-vary across a protein family. Mutual information confounds direct and indirect couplings; a Potts model p(s) ∝ exp(Σ h_i(s_i) + Σ J_ij(s_i, s_j)) disentangles them. plmDCA fits it by maximising the pseudo-likelihood with ℓ₂ penalties (one softmax regression per column), ranks pairs by the Frobenius norm of J_ij, and applies the average product correction (APC) to remove background.
- **Block:** `experimental.bio.PottsModel` (pseudo-likelihood fit by L-BFGS or Adam, Gibbs sampling, Frobenius scores, APC) and `experimental.bio.CoevolutionScores` (MI, MI+APC).
- **Experiment:** `StructureAndPhylogeny` (contact table).
- **Toy task:** `BioSequences` Potts samples: L = 30–60 positions, q = 4–21 states, a planted sparse contact map (chain-like plus long-range pairs), 200–2,000 sequences, with phylogenetic correlation as a stress test.
- **Baselines:** MI and MI+APC; a lasso on one-hot pairs (12.1.1 if landed, else a local stand-in).
- **Expected metric:** precision of the top-L predicted contacts vs number of sequences. Hypothesis: plmDCA+APC reaches top-L precision 0.8+ at 1,000 sequences where MI+APC stays at 0.4–0.5; phylogenetic correlation lowers all methods but the ranking holds.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1211.1281](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.1281); Weigt et al., "Identification of direct residue contacts in protein–protein interaction by message passing" (PNAS 2009).

### 12.4.6 RNA secondary structure: Nussinov, a nearest-neighbour energy model, McCaskill base-pair probabilities and a learned CRF scorer (Nussinov & Jacobson, PNAS 1980; Zuker & Stiegler, NAR 1981; McCaskill, Biopolymers 1990; Do, Woods & Batzoglou, Bioinformatics 2006)
Nested secondary structures are context-free, so O(n³) dynamic programs find them: Nussinov maximises base pairs, Zuker minimises a loop-based free energy (stacking rewards, loop penalties), and McCaskill's inside–outside recursion gives the partition function and base-pair probabilities, from which maximum-expected-accuracy structures are decoded. CONTRAfold learns the loop-model weights discriminatively as a CRF from known structures.
- **Block:** `experimental.bio.RnaFolding` (NUSSINOV, SIMPLE_ZUKER with a small stacking/loop table, MCCASKILL partition function and MEA decoding, CRF_TRAINING of the scoring weights by gradient of the log partition function).
- **Experiment:** `StructureAndPhylogeny` (folding table).
- **Toy task:** `BioSequences` RNA sequences of length 30–120 with planted nested stems (and pseudoknot-free ground truth), generated by an energy model that the methods do not know exactly.
- **Baselines:** Nussinov; a 1-D CNN predicting the pairing matrix with a symmetric output and greedy decoding (an unconstrained learner); `ChartParser`'s inside–outside code is not reused but is the conceptual reference.
- **Expected metric:** base-pair F1 and the fraction of predicted structures that are valid (nested, canonical). Hypothesis: the energy model beats Nussinov by 0.2–0.3 F1; the learned CRF beats the fixed energy model when the generator's energies differ; the unconstrained CNN produces invalid structures and loses after decoding.
- **Source:** McCaskill, "The equilibrium partition function and base pair binding probabilities for RNA secondary structure" (Biopolymers 1990); Do, Woods & Batzoglou, "CONTRAfold" (Bioinformatics 2006).

### 12.4.7 Channel decoding: belief propagation and min-sum on Tanner graphs, neural BP with learned weights, and Viterbi for convolutional codes (Gallager, 1962; Fossorier et al., IEEE T-Com 1999; Nachmani, Be'ery & Burshtein, Allerton 2016; Viterbi, IEEE T-IT 1967)
Linear block codes are decoded by message passing on the parity-check (Tanner) graph: sum-product BP is optimal on trees and good on sparse LDPC graphs; min-sum approximates it cheaply (normalised and offset variants fix its overestimate). Short high-density codes (BCH) have many short cycles, where BP is weak; neural BP unrolls T iterations and learns a weight per edge (and per iteration) by training on noisy all-zero codewords, gaining up to about 1 dB. Convolutional codes are decoded by the Viterbi algorithm on the trellis.
- **Block:** `experimental.comms.ChannelDecoders` (HARD_SYNDROME, BP, MIN_SUM, NORMALIZED_MIN_SUM, NEURAL_BP, NEURAL_MIN_SUM, VITERBI for convolutional codes) with BPSK over AWGN.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.communications.ChannelDecoding`.
- **Toy task:** `ChannelCodes` Hamming(7,4), BCH(31,16), BCH(63,45), a regular LDPC and a rate-1/2 K = 3 convolutional code; Eb/N0 from 1 to 6 dB.
- **Baselines:** hard-decision syndrome decoding; maximum-likelihood decoding by enumeration for Hamming(7,4) and BCH(31,16) (the optimum); uncoded BPSK.
- **Expected metric:** BER and FER vs Eb/N0, and the gap to ML in dB at FER 10⁻². Hypothesis: BP is within 0.3 dB of ML on the LDPC code but 1–2 dB away on BCH; neural BP closes 0.5–1 dB of the BCH gap; Viterbi gives about 4–5 dB coding gain at BER 10⁻⁴ for the convolutional code.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1607.04793](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.04793)

### 12.4.8 Transformer decoders for error-correcting codes: ECCT and CrossMPT (Choukroun & Wolf, NeurIPS 2022; Park et al., ICLR 2025)
ECCT feeds the channel magnitudes |y| and the syndrome as tokens to a transformer whose self-attention is masked by the code's parity-check structure, and predicts the multiplicative noise; it is code-aware but model-free. CrossMPT (2025) treats magnitudes and syndrome as two separate sequences and alternately updates each by masked cross-attention (H masks one direction, Hᵀ the other), which mimics message passing; with shared weights it has ECCT's parameter count and lower error rates.
- **Block:** `experimental.comms.TransformerDecoders` (ECCT, CROSS_MPT), on core attention with explicit masks.
- **Experiment:** `ChannelDecoding` (learned-decoder table).
- **Toy task:** `ChannelCodes` Hamming(7,4), BCH(31,16) and the small LDPC code, trained on random noise realisations at 3–6 dB for a few hundred steps.
- **Baselines:** 12.4.7's BP and neural BP; ML decoding on the smallest codes.
- **Expected metric:** BER (as −ln BER, the papers' reporting unit) at 4, 5 and 6 dB, and parameter count. Hypothesis: ECCT beats BP on BCH(31,16) at this size; CrossMPT beats ECCT at equal parameters; on the LDPC code neither beats tuned neural BP at the test budget.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2203.14966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.14966), [arXiv:2405.01033](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01033)

### 12.4.9 End-to-end communication autoencoders: learned constellations over AWGN and fading channels (O'Shea & Hoydis, IEEE TCCN 2017; Dörner et al., IEEE JSAC 2018)
A transmitter network maps one of M messages to n channel uses under a power constraint, the channel adds noise (or fading), and a receiver network classifies the message; training end to end with cross-entropy learns constellations and codes jointly. On AWGN with (M, n) = (16, 7) the learned system matches or beats Hamming-coded BPSK with hard decisions; on Rayleigh fading it learns diversity.
- **Block:** `experimental.comms.CommunicationAutoencoder` (dense transmitter and receiver, energy normalisation, AWGN and Rayleigh channel layers, a learned 2-D constellation mode).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.communications.LearnedTransceivers`.
- **Toy task:** (M, n) ∈ {(4, 2), (16, 2), (16, 7)}, Eb/N0 0–10 dB; 2-D constellations of 16 points.
- **Baselines:** BPSK, QPSK and 16-QAM with ML detection; Hamming(7,4) + BPSK with hard and soft (ML) decoding (12.4.7).
- **Expected metric:** block error rate vs Eb/N0, and the learned constellation's minimum distance. Hypothesis: the (16, 7) autoencoder matches soft-decision Hamming(7,4) and beats hard decision by about 1.5 dB; the learned 16-point constellation is hexagonal-like and beats 16-QAM by a fraction of a dB.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1702.00832](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.00832)

### 12.4.10 MIMO detection: zero-forcing, MMSE, sphere decoding and unfolded DetNet (Paulraj et al., 2003; Hassibi & Vikalo, IEEE TSP 2005; Samuel, Diskin & Wiesel, SPAWC 2017 and IEEE TSP 2019)
With y = Hx + n and x from a QAM alphabet, maximum-likelihood detection is a closest-lattice-point problem; zero-forcing and MMSE invert H linearly and then round (fast, poor when H is ill-conditioned); sphere decoding enumerates lattice points inside a shrinking radius with a tree search and is exact at a variable cost. DetNet unfolds projected gradient descent on ‖y − Hx‖² into a network with learned step sizes and nonlinear projections, trained over random H.
- **Block:** `experimental.comms.MimoDetectors` (ZERO_FORCING, MMSE, SIC, SPHERE_DECODER, DETNET).
- **Experiment:** `LearnedTransceivers` (MIMO table).
- **Toy task:** `ChannelCodes` Rayleigh channels with 4×4 and 8×8 antennas, BPSK/QPSK, SNR 0–20 dB, and an ill-conditioned (correlated) variant.
- **Baselines:** ZF, MMSE and MMSE-SIC; sphere decoding as the ML reference.
- **Expected metric:** symbol error rate vs SNR and average complexity (nodes visited or FLOPs). Hypothesis: DetNet gets within 1 dB of ML on i.i.d. channels and beats MMSE by 3+ dB at high SNR; on correlated channels the gap to ML widens; the sphere decoder's cost grows sharply at low SNR.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1706.01151](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.01151)

### 12.4.11 Sparse recovery: OMP, ISTA/FISTA and approximate message passing, with the phase transition (Pati et al., 1993; Daubechies et al., CPAM 2004; Beck & Teboulle, SIAM J. Imaging Sci. 2009; Donoho, Maleki & Montanari, PNAS 2009)
Compressed sensing recovers an s-sparse x from m < n random measurements y = Ax. OMP greedily adds the column most correlated with the residual; ISTA alternates gradient steps with soft-thresholding and FISTA accelerates it; AMP adds an Onsager correction term that makes the effective noise Gaussian, giving fast convergence and a sharp, predictable phase transition in (m/n, s/m).
- **Block:** `experimental.signal.SparseRecovery` (OMP, ISTA, FISTA, AMP with soft thresholding and a tuned threshold schedule, and the Donoho–Tanner phase-transition sweep).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.communications.SparseRecovery`.
- **Toy task:** Gaussian A with n = 256–512, sampling ratio m/n ∈ [0.1, 0.7], sparsity ratio s/m ∈ [0.05, 0.6], noise σ ∈ {0, 0.01}.
- **Baselines:** least squares (minimum norm); `LastLayerRetraining`'s ISTA solver (as an implementation check); 12.1.1's lasso (cross-WP soft link, local stand-in until it lands).
- **Expected metric:** probability of exact recovery over the phase diagram, NMSE, and iterations to 1e-4. Hypothesis: AMP reaches the ℓ₁ phase transition in about 20 iterations where ISTA needs hundreds; OMP is best at very low sparsity and fails earlier as s grows; minimum-norm LS never recovers.
- **Source:** [arXiv:0907.3574](https://arxiv.org/abs/0907.3574)

### 12.4.12 Unrolled sparse solvers: LISTA, ALISTA and learned AMP (Gregor & LeCun, ICML 2010; Liu et al., ICLR 2019; Borgerding, Schniter & Rangan, IEEE TSP 2017)
LISTA unrolls T ISTA iterations into a network and learns the matrices and thresholds, reaching in 10–16 layers what ISTA needs hundreds of iterations for. ALISTA shows that the weight matrices can be computed analytically from A (by a coherence-minimising optimisation) and only thresholds and step sizes need learning; learned AMP (LAMP) unrolls AMP with its Onsager term and learns per-layer denoiser parameters.
- **Block:** `experimental.signal.UnrolledSolvers` (LISTA, LISTA_CP with coupled weights, ALISTA, LAMP).
- **Experiment:** `SparseRecovery` (unrolled table).
- **Toy task:** 12.4.11's problems at a fixed A (m = 128, n = 256, s = 10–20), training on 10⁴ random sparse vectors, testing in- and out-of-distribution (different sparsity, noise).
- **Baselines:** 12.4.11's ISTA, FISTA and AMP at equal iterations/layers.
- **Expected metric:** NMSE vs layers/iterations, and robustness to a sparsity shift. Hypothesis: LISTA and LAMP reach −30 dB NMSE in 12–16 layers where FISTA needs about 10× more iterations; ALISTA matches LISTA with 100× fewer learned parameters; all learned solvers degrade when sparsity doubles while AMP adapts.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1612.01183](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01183); Liu, Chen, Wang & Yin, "ALISTA" (ICLR 2019).

### 12.4.13 Dictionary learning for image patches: K-SVD and online dictionary learning (Aharon, Elad & Bruckstein, IEEE TSP 2006; Mairal et al., ICML 2009 and JMLR 2010)
Sparse coding represents each patch as a sparse combination of dictionary atoms. K-SVD alternates OMP coding with a rank-1 SVD update of each atom over the patches that use it; online dictionary learning (Mairal) updates the dictionary by block coordinate descent on accumulated sufficient statistics from mini-batches, scaling to millions of patches. Learned dictionaries denoise patches by sparse coding with a noise-dependent error target.
- **Block:** `experimental.signal.DictionaryLearning` (K_SVD, ONLINE_DL, patch denoising with overlapping-patch averaging), using 12.4.11's OMP.
- **Experiment:** `SparseRecovery` (dictionary table).
- **Toy task:** 8×8 patches from `ShapeImages` and digits, Gaussian noise σ ∈ {0.1, 0.2}, dictionaries of 64–256 atoms.
- **Baselines:** an overcomplete DCT dictionary; PCA truncation; wave 10's DnCNN-style denoiser from `experimental.restore.Denoisers`; `SuperpositionRecovery`'s SAE-style dictionary (read-only, as a reference).
- **Expected metric:** denoising PSNR and recovery of planted atoms (on synthetic data generated from a known dictionary). Hypothesis: K-SVD beats the DCT dictionary by 0.5–1.5 dB and recovers > 90% of planted atoms; online DL matches K-SVD at 5–10× less time; the trained CNN denoiser wins when trained on the same noise level.
- **Source:** [arXiv:0908.0050](https://arxiv.org/abs/0908.0050); Aharon, Elad & Bruckstein, "K-SVD" (IEEE TSP 2006).

### 12.4.14 Robust PCA by principal component pursuit, for background subtraction and corrupted matrices (Candès, Li, Ma & Wright, JACM 2011; Lin, Chen & Ma, 2010)
PCP splits a matrix into a low-rank part and a sparse part by minimising ‖L‖_* + λ‖S‖₁ subject to L + S = M, solved by an inexact augmented Lagrangian (singular-value thresholding for L, soft-thresholding for S); with λ = 1/√max(m, n) it recovers both exactly under incoherence. It is the classic for video background subtraction, where the static background is low-rank and moving objects are sparse.
- **Block:** `experimental.signal.RobustPca` (PCP by inexact ALM, a non-convex alternating-projection variant), reusing the singular-value thresholding idea of `TriplyRobustPanel` (calling it if exposed, otherwise a local SVT on `ThinSvd`).
- **Experiment:** `SparseRecovery` (RPCA table).
- **Toy task:** random low-rank (r = 2–10) plus sparse (1–30% entries, large magnitude) matrices; `ShapeClips` videos with a static textured background and moving shapes.
- **Baselines:** PCA truncated at the true rank; a median-background model for video; the wave 11 panel matrix completion (read-only) on the same matrices with the corrupted entries marked missing (an oracle).
- **Expected metric:** relative error of L, F1 of the sparse support, and foreground-mask IoU for video. Hypothesis: PCP recovers L exactly up to 10–20% corruption where PCA's error grows linearly; on video it beats the median background on IoU when objects move slowly.
- **Source:** [arXiv:0912.3599](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.3599)

### 12.4.15 Volatility models: GARCH(1,1), GJR-GARCH and EGARCH with Student-t innovations (Bollerslev, J. Econometrics 1986; Glosten, Jagannathan & Runkle, J. Finance 1993; Nelson, Econometrica 1991)
Returns are nearly unpredictable but their variance clusters. GARCH(1,1) models σ²_t = ω + α ε²_{t−1} + β σ²_{t−1}, fitted by maximum likelihood with Gaussian or Student-t innovations; GJR adds a leverage term for negative shocks, EGARCH models log-variance so no positivity constraints are needed. They are the standard against which ML volatility forecasts must be judged, using a robust loss such as QLIKE.
- **Block:** `experimental.markets.Volatility` (EWMA, GARCH, GJR_GARCH, EGARCH; Gaussian and t likelihoods via core `Distributions` if landed, else `discovery.Distributions`; multi-step variance forecasts).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.markets.RiskAndHedging`.
- **Toy task:** `MarketPaths` returns from GARCH, GJR, Heston and regime-switching processes (1,000–5,000 days), with the true conditional variance kept.
- **Baselines:** RiskMetrics EWMA (λ = 0.94); rolling historical variance; an MLP or GRU on past squared returns (wave 8 forecasting lineup style).
- **Expected metric:** QLIKE and MSE of variance forecasts at horizons 1, 5 and 20, and 99% VaR exceedance rates. Hypothesis: the matched GARCH family wins QLIKE on its own process, GJR wins under leverage, EWMA is close behind; the neural model needs several thousand days to tie GARCH and loses at short samples; Gaussian innovations under-cover VaR on t data.
- **Source:** Bollerslev, "Generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity" (J. Econometrics 1986).

### 12.4.16 Deep hedging with transaction costs and convex risk measures (Buehler, Gonon, Teichmann & Wood, Quantitative Finance 2019)
Deep hedging trains a network that maps the market state (time, price, current holding, volatility estimate) to the next hedge position, minimising a convex risk measure (CVaR or entropic risk) of the hedged P&L over simulated paths, with proportional transaction costs. Without costs and in Black–Scholes it recovers the delta hedge; with costs it learns a no-trade band; under stochastic volatility it uses the variance information.
- **Block:** `experimental.markets.DeepHedging` (recurrent or feed-forward policy, CVaR and entropic objectives with the Rockafellar–Uryasev auxiliary variable, proportional costs, indifference price).
- **Experiment:** `RiskAndHedging` (hedging table).
- **Toy task:** `MarketPaths` GBM and Heston paths (30 rebalancing dates, 10⁴ training paths), a European call, costs 0–1%.
- **Baselines:** no hedge; Black–Scholes delta hedging (with daily rebalancing); delta hedging with a Whalley–Wilmott no-trade band.
- **Expected metric:** CVaR₉₅ and standard deviation of hedged P&L, and total transaction costs. Hypothesis: without costs deep hedging matches BS delta within noise; at 1% costs it cuts CVaR by 10–30% versus delta hedging by trading less; on Heston it beats the BS delta that uses the wrong volatility.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1802.03042](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03042)

### 12.4.17 Learned auctions: RegretNet and RegretFormer (Dütting et al., ICML 2019; Ivanov et al., NeurIPS 2022)
Optimal multi-item auctions are unknown in general. RegretNet parameterises the allocation and payment rules with networks, maximises expected revenue, and enforces approximate incentive compatibility through an augmented Lagrangian on each bidder's ex-post regret, measured by gradient-based misreport search. RegretFormer replaces the MLPs with attention over bidders and items (permutation-equivariant) and adds a loss with an explicit regret budget, giving higher revenue at the same regret.
- **Block:** `experimental.markets.LearnedAuctions` (REGRET_NET, REGRET_FORMER; misreport inner loop; revenue and regret metrics).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.markets.MechanismDesign`.
- **Toy task:** `MarketPaths` private values: 1 bidder 2 items U[0,1] (the Manelli–Vincent optimum is known), 2 bidders 2 items, and 1 item n bidders (Myerson's optimum known).
- **Baselines:** second-price auction with and without the optimal reserve (as in `BudgetPacer`'s auction model); Myerson's optimal auction for one item; separate per-item Myerson; the Manelli–Vincent mechanism.
- **Expected metric:** revenue and maximum/mean regret. Hypothesis: RegretNet recovers Myerson's revenue within 2% for one item and the Manelli–Vincent revenue (≈ 0.55) within 3% with regret < 0.005; RegretFormer matches or exceeds RegretNet's revenue at equal regret on 2×2.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1706.03459](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03459), [arXiv:2202.13110](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.13110)

### 12.4.18 Online portfolio selection: Cover's universal portfolio, exponentiated gradient, online Newton step and OLMAR (Cover, Mathematical Finance 1991; Helmbold et al., Mathematical Finance 1998; Agarwal et al., ICML 2006; Li & Hoi, ICML 2012)
Online portfolio selection rebalances wealth among m assets each period to compete with the best constant-rebalanced portfolio (BCRP) in hindsight. The universal portfolio averages all CRPs weighted by their wealth and has regret O(m log T); exponentiated gradient is a cheap multiplicative update; ONS uses second-order information for log T regret with better constants; OLMAR is a "follow-the-loser" strategy that bets on mean reversion of moving averages.
- **Block:** `experimental.markets.OnlinePortfolios` (UNIFORM_BUY_AND_HOLD, UCRP, UNIVERSAL by Monte Carlo over the simplex, EG, ONS, OLMAR; transaction costs).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.markets.PortfolioSelection`.
- **Toy task:** `MarketPaths` multi-asset GBM (no edge), mean-reverting paths (OLMAR's regime) and momentum paths, 5–20 assets, 1,000–2,000 periods, costs 0–0.2%.
- **Baselines:** buy-and-hold; uniform CRP; BCRP in hindsight (computed by EG to convergence).
- **Expected metric:** log wealth and regret to BCRP, with and without costs. Hypothesis: universal, EG and ONS have small, sublinear regret on every regime; OLMAR wins by a large margin on mean-reverting paths and loses on momentum paths; costs erase OLMAR's edge at 0.2%.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1206.4626](https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.4626); Agarwal, Hazan, Kale & Schapire, "Algorithms for portfolio management based on the Newton method" (ICML 2006).

### 12.5.1 Discrete gradient estimators: REINFORCE with baselines and leave-one-out, REBAR, RELAX, ARM/DisARM and the discrete Stein estimator RODEO (Williams, 1992; Kool et al., 2019; Tucker et al., NeurIPS 2017; Grathwohl et al., ICLR 2018; Yin & Zhou, ICLR 2019; Dong, Mnih & Tucker, NeurIPS 2020; Shi et al., ICML 2022)
Gradients through discrete samples are either biased (straight-through, Gumbel-softmax) or high-variance (REINFORCE). Leave-one-out baselines use the other samples' mean; REBAR uses a Gumbel-softmax relaxation as a control variate with an adaptive temperature; RELAX learns the control variate with a small network; ARM antithetically couples two Bernoulli samples through a shared uniform and DisARM integrates out the noise further; RODEO builds control variates from discrete Stein operators applied to Markov chain kernels, cutting variance further at the same number of function evaluations.
- **Block:** `experimental.estimators.DiscreteGradients` (REINFORCE, RLOO, STRAIGHT_THROUGH, GUMBEL_ST, REBAR, RELAX, ARM, DISARM, RODEO for Bernoulli and categorical variables).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.estimation.GradientEstimators`.
- **Toy task:** the toy objective E[(b − 0.499)²] over Bernoulli b (a hard sign test); a binary-latent VAE on binarised digits (200 latent bits); a categorical latent (k = 10) VAE.
- **Baselines:** Gumbel-softmax and straight-through as used in `CommunicatingAgents`/quantisers (core-adjacent code, read-only); exact gradients on the toy objective.
- **Expected metric:** gradient variance (log-scale) and bias at equal function evaluations, and the VAE ELBO after a fixed budget. Hypothesis: on the toy objective ST/Gumbel get the sign wrong while unbiased estimators do not; DisARM and RODEO have 2–10× lower variance than RLOO; on the VAE, RLOO/DisARM/RODEO reach better ELBOs than REINFORCE, and Gumbel-ST is competitive only at small budgets.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1703.07370](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.07370), [arXiv:1711.00123](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00123), [arXiv:1807.11143](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.11143), [arXiv:2006.10680](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10680), [arXiv:2202.09497](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09497)

### 12.5.2 Differentiable sorting and ranking: NeuralSort, SoftSort, fast soft sort and rank by isotonic projection, and monotonic differentiable sorting networks (Grover et al., ICLR 2019; Prillo & Eisenschlos, ICML 2020; Blondel et al., ICML 2020; Petersen et al., ICLR 2022)
Sorting is piecewise constant, so its gradient is zero. NeuralSort relaxes the permutation matrix to a unimodal row-stochastic matrix with a temperature; SoftSort is a simpler softmax over |sorted s − s|; Blondel et al. cast sort and rank as projections onto the permutahedron solved by pool-adjacent-violators in O(n log n); differentiable sorting networks relax each compare-and-swap with a sigmoid, and monotonic sigmoids guarantee gradients of the right sign.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.SoftSorting` (NEURAL_SORT, SOFT_SORT, FAST_SOFT_SORT/RANK, SORTING_NETWORK with odd-even and bitonic layouts and monotonic sigmoids; differentiable top-k).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.estimation.DifferentiableAlgorithms`.
- **Toy task:** `DecisionInstances` sorting of 4–9 multi-digit images supervised only by the ranking (the MNIST-sort benchmark at toy scale); a differentiable top-k kNN classifier on digits.
- **Baselines:** Sinkhorn-based sorting with `EntropicTransport` (read-only); ListNet/pairwise ranking losses from the recsys learning-to-rank code; a per-image regression on the true values (an oracle with stronger supervision).
- **Expected metric:** exact-ranking accuracy and per-element accuracy at n = 5 and 9, and time per step. Hypothesis: monotonic sorting networks and fast soft rank match or beat NeuralSort at n = 9; SoftSort is the simplest and nearly as good; Sinkhorn sorting is the slowest; all fall below the regression oracle by a few points.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1903.08850](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08850), [arXiv:2006.16038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16038), [arXiv:2002.08871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08871), [arXiv:2203.09630](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.09630)

### 12.5.3 Learning through combinatorial solvers: perturbed optimisers, Fenchel–Young losses and blackbox differentiation (Berthet et al., NeurIPS 2020; Blondel, Martins & Niculae, JMLR 2020; Vlastelica, Paulus et al., ICLR 2020)
To train a network whose output feeds an argmax-type solver (shortest path, ranking, matching), perturbed optimisers average the solver's output under Gaussian perturbations of its input, which gives a smooth map with Monte Carlo gradients; the associated Fenchel–Young loss has a simple gradient (perturbed solution minus target). Blackbox differentiation instead calls the solver once more on costs nudged by the incoming gradient and uses the finite difference of the two solutions as the gradient of a piecewise-linear interpolation.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.PerturbedOptimizers` (PERTURBED_ARGMAX with Gaussian/Gumbel noise, FENCHEL_YOUNG loss, BLACKBOX_DIFFERENTIATION with λ), wrapping solvers: grid Dijkstra (12.3.11's if landed, else local), ranking (sort), and assignment (`MinibatchOptimalTransport`'s Hungarian, read-only); sparsemax loss reuses `Sparsemax`.
- **Experiment:** `DifferentiableAlgorithms` (solver-in-the-loop table).
- **Toy task:** `DecisionInstances` "Warcraft-lite" shortest paths: 8×8 or 12×12 grids of terrain patches, a CNN predicts per-cell costs, supervised by the optimal path only.
- **Baselines:** a CNN trained to predict the path cells directly (multi-label BCE); a CNN regressing the true costs (needs cost labels, an oracle).
- **Expected metric:** fraction of optimal paths and normalised cost regret. Hypothesis: perturbed Fenchel–Young and blackbox differentiation beat direct path prediction by 20+ points in optimal-path rate; Fenchel–Young is the most stable across λ/σ; the cost-regression oracle is no better than them despite more supervision.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2002.08676](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08676), [arXiv:1901.02324](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.02324), [arXiv:1912.02175](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.02175)

### 12.5.4 Decision-focused learning: SPO+ and perturbation-gradient losses for predict-then-optimise (Elmachtoub & Grigas, Management Science 2022; Huang & Gupta, NeurIPS 2024)
When predictions are only used to choose decisions by an optimisation problem, the right loss is decision regret, not prediction MSE. SPO+ is a convex surrogate of the regret of a linear objective, with a subgradient computable by one extra solver call. Perturbation-gradient (PG) losses approximate the derivative of the plug-in policy's value in the direction of the outcome by finite differences (backward or central), which by Danskin's theorem equals the decision loss; they are Lipschitz and differentiable and stay best-in-class under misspecification, where SPO+ need not.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.DecisionFocused` (TWO_STAGE_MSE, SPO_PLUS, PG_BACKWARD, PG_CENTRAL, plus 12.5.3's perturbed Fenchel–Young as a row), with linear and MLP predictors, solving over `LinearProgram` and the wave 11 `BranchAndBound` (read-only).
- **Experiment:** `DifferentiableAlgorithms` (decision table).
- **Toy task:** `DecisionInstances` shortest path on a 5×5 grid with costs from a degree-d polynomial of features (d = 1, 2, 4, 6: increasing misspecification), multi-dimensional knapsack and a portfolio LP; 100–1,000 training samples.
- **Baselines:** two-stage least squares and two-stage random forest (`RegressionForest`); the true-cost oracle.
- **Expected metric:** normalised regret on test instances vs training size and misspecification degree. Hypothesis: at d = 1 two-stage MSE is as good as any; as d grows, SPO+ and PG cut regret by 20–50% versus two-stage linear; PG beats SPO+ at the highest misspecification; the random-forest two-stage is competitive with enough data.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1710.08005](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.08005), [arXiv:2402.03256](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03256)

### 12.5.5 Differentiable quadratic-program layers: OptNet (Amos & Kolter, ICML 2017)
OptNet layers solve a QP min ½zᵀQz + qᵀz s.t. Az = b, Gz ≤ h in the forward pass (a batched primal–dual interior-point method) and differentiate through the KKT conditions in the backward pass, so constraints themselves (A, G, h) can be learned. The paper's showcase learns the constraints of 4×4 Sudoku from examples, without being told the rules.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.QpLayer` (dense primal–dual interior point, implicit KKT backward, learnable Q, q, G, h, A, b).
- **Experiment:** `DifferentiableAlgorithms` (QP-layer table).
- **Toy task:** `DecisionInstances` 4×4 Sudoku completions (one-hot relaxation), and a learned projection onto an unknown polytope.
- **Baselines:** `SatNetLayer` (wave 7, read-only) on the same Sudoku; an MLP of matching parameter count; the exact projection (oracle) for the polytope task.
- **Expected metric:** puzzle accuracy (all cells right) on held-out puzzles, and constraint violation of outputs. Hypothesis: OptNet and SATNet both learn 4×4 Sudoku to > 90% puzzle accuracy where the MLP stays below 30%; OptNet's outputs satisfy the learned constraints exactly; OptNet is slower per step than SATNet.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1703.00443](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00443)

### 12.5.6 Implicit hypergradients: the implicit function theorem with Neumann-series inverses, against unrolled differentiation (Lorraine, Vicol & Duvenaud, AISTATS 2020; Shaban et al., AISTATS 2019; Pedregosa, ICML 2016)
Hyperparameters that enter the training loss (per-parameter weight decay, data weights, augmentation strengths) can be optimised by gradient: at a training optimum, the IFT gives dL_val/dλ = −∂L_val/∂w · H⁻¹ · ∂²L_train/∂w∂λ, and the inverse-Hessian-vector product is approximated by a truncated Neumann series of Hessian-vector products. Unrolled and truncated backpropagation through the last K training steps is the alternative; the IFT method scales to millions of hyperparameters with constant memory.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.Hypergradients` (IFT with NEUMANN and CG inverse, UNROLLED_K, TRUNCATED_K), alternating with an inner optimiser.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.estimation.OptimisationMethods`.
- **Toy task:** per-feature weight decay on a linear/MLP regressor with 200 noisy features (only 10 useful), and per-example data weights on `LabelCorruption` digits with 30% flipped labels.
- **Baselines:** grid and random search over a single global weight decay; GP Bayesian optimisation (core `HyperparameterSearch`); `DartsSearch`'s first-order bilevel step (read-only, as a reference); a clean-data oracle for the reweighting task.
- **Expected metric:** validation and test loss vs hypergradient steps, and (reweighting) the AUROC of learned weights for detecting flipped labels. Hypothesis: per-feature IFT decay beats the best global decay by 10–30% in test MSE; Neumann with 5–20 terms matches CG; learned data weights flag flipped labels with AUROC > 0.9.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1911.02590](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.02590)

### 12.5.7 Variance-reduced stochastic optimisation: SVRG, SAGA and SARAH (Johnson & Zhang, NeurIPS 2013; Defazio, Bach & Lacoste-Julien, NeurIPS 2014; Nguyen et al., ICML 2017)
For finite sums, SGD's gradient noise forces decreasing steps and sublinear convergence. SVRG computes a full gradient at a snapshot every epoch and corrects each stochastic gradient by the difference at the snapshot; SAGA keeps a table of the last gradient of every example and uses the table average; SARAH uses a recursive estimator. All converge linearly on strongly convex problems at a constant step size; on neural networks their advantage is known to vanish.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.VarianceReduced` (SGD, SVRG, SAGA, SARAH, with optional mini-batching), on vectors and on `ParameterVector`-style flattened MLP parameters.
- **Experiment:** `OptimisationMethods` (finite-sum table).
- **Toy task:** `DecisionInstances` finite-sum least squares and ℓ₂-logistic regression (n = 1,000–10,000, condition numbers 10–10⁴), and a small MLP on digits.
- **Baselines:** SGD with tuned decay; Adam; full-batch L-BFGS (wave 7).
- **Expected metric:** suboptimality vs gradient evaluations (epochs). Hypothesis: SVRG/SAGA reach 1e-8 suboptimality in 20–50 epochs where SGD stalls at 1e-3; L-BFGS wins at moderate conditioning; on the MLP, SVRG gives no gain over SGD/Adam (as Defazio & Bottou reported).
- **Source:** [arXiv:1407.0202](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.0202), [arXiv:1703.00102](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00102)

### 12.5.8 Projection-free and proximal methods: Frank–Wolfe with away steps, proximal gradient and ADMM (Frank & Wolfe, 1956; Jaggi, ICML 2013; Lacoste-Julien & Jaggi, NeurIPS 2015; Boyd et al., 2011)
Frank–Wolfe replaces projection by a linear minimisation oracle over the feasible set (a vertex of the simplex, a rank-one matrix for the trace-norm ball), so iterates are sparse or low-rank combinations of atoms; away steps and pairwise steps remove bad atoms and give linear convergence on polytopes. Proximal gradient (FISTA) handles non-smooth regularisers with a prox; ADMM splits a problem into easy pieces with a dual update. They are compared on the same constrained problems.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.ConstrainedSolvers` (FRANK_WOLFE, AWAY_STEP_FW, PAIRWISE_FW, PROJECTED_GRADIENT, FISTA, ADMM) over simplex, ℓ₁-ball and trace-norm-ball constraints.
- **Experiment:** `OptimisationMethods` (constrained table).
- **Toy task:** simplex-constrained least squares (portfolio-like), ℓ₁-ball regression, and trace-norm matrix completion on a `SyntheticRecsys` rating matrix.
- **Baselines:** projected gradient; `ParetoDescent`'s internal Frank–Wolfe (read-only, as an implementation check).
- **Expected metric:** suboptimality vs time, and solution sparsity/rank. Hypothesis: vanilla FW converges sublinearly and away/pairwise FW linearly on the simplex; for trace-norm completion FW is 5–10× faster per iteration than projected gradient (rank-one LMO vs full SVD) and keeps low-rank iterates.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1511.05932](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05932); Jaggi, "Revisiting Frank–Wolfe" (ICML 2013).

### 12.5.9 Learned optimisers: an LSTM L2O and Celo (Andrychowicz et al., NeurIPS 2016; Moudgil et al., TMLR 2025)
A learned optimiser is a small network that maps per-parameter features (gradient, momentum, parameter value) to updates, meta-trained on a distribution of training tasks by unrolling. The LSTM optimiser of 2016 is coordinate-wise and trained by truncated backprop; Celo (2025) combines a per-parameter MLP update rule scaled by tensor norms with an LSTM scheduler over global loss features that outputs an exponential step-size multiplier, meta-trains on a handful of small MLP tasks with truncated persistent evolution strategies, augments tasks by random parameter rescaling, and trains the rule first and the scheduler second; it reports strong generalisation from very little meta-training compute.
- **Block:** `experimental.diffopt.LearnedOptimizers` (LSTM_L2O with truncated BPTT, CELO_LITE with the two-stage recipe, task augmentation and truncated PES), using core `Gradients.clipByGlobalNorm` if landed for meta-gradient clipping.
- **Experiment:** `OptimisationMethods` (learned-optimiser table).
- **Toy task:** meta-train on random quadratics and 2–3 tiny MLP tasks on `ToyTasks` spirals (a few seconds of meta-training); meta-test on unseen tasks: wider MLPs, digits MLP, a small CNN, and 5× longer horizons.
- **Baselines:** Adam with a tuned learning rate; `DAdaptation` and Schedule-Free (tuning-free); `MeZo` is not relevant (zeroth-order).
- **Expected metric:** meta-test loss after 200 and 1,000 steps relative to tuned Adam, and failure rate (divergence) on out-of-distribution tasks. Hypothesis: both learned optimisers beat Adam on in-distribution tasks; the LSTM L2O diverges on longer horizons and wider nets; Celo-lite stays within 10% of tuned Adam out of distribution and beats untuned Adam; task augmentation is the ablation that matters most.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1606.04474](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04474), [arXiv:2501.12670](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12670)

### 12.5.10 Expectation propagation for GP classification (Minka, UAI 2001; Rasmussen & Williams, 2006; Kuss & Rasmussen, JMLR 2005)
EP approximates a non-Gaussian posterior by a Gaussian built from site approximations: each site is updated in turn by removing it (cavity), moment-matching the tilted distribution with the true likelihood factor, and putting it back. For probit GP classification the moments are closed form; EP is known to give better-calibrated predictive probabilities and marginal likelihoods than the Laplace approximation, which centres on the mode and underestimates variance.
- **Block:** `experimental.estimators.ExpectationPropagation` (parallel and sequential EP for probit likelihoods, damping, EP marginal likelihood for hyperparameters), on `GaussianProcess` kernels.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.estimation.InferenceAndTesting`.
- **Toy task:** 2-D binary tasks (moons, a noisy XOR, a class-imbalanced blob), 100–500 points; posterior "truth" from long HMC runs (wave 7 HMC on the latent function).
- **Baselines:** Laplace GP classification (`GaussianProcess`, wave 6); SVGP (wave 6); logistic regression on RFF features.
- **Expected metric:** test NLL, ECE, and KL from the HMC posterior marginals; hyperparameter optima. Hypothesis: EP's predictive NLL and marginals are closer to HMC than Laplace's, with the largest gain on noisy XOR; EP and Laplace have equal accuracy.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1301.2294](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.2294); Kuss & Rasmussen, "Assessing approximate inference for binary Gaussian process classification" (JMLR 2005).

### 12.5.11 Nested sampling for evidence and posteriors (Skilling, Bayesian Analysis 2006; Feroz, Hobson & Bridges, MNRAS 2009; Handley et al., MNRAS 2015)
Nested sampling turns the evidence integral into a one-dimensional integral over prior mass: keep N live points, repeatedly replace the lowest-likelihood point by a prior sample with higher likelihood, and shrink the enclosed prior mass by about e^(−1/N) each step; the dead points with their weights give the evidence and posterior samples. Constrained sampling uses ellipsoids (MultiNest) or slice sampling (PolyChord-style) inside the likelihood contour. It handles multimodal posteriors and gives an error bar on log Z from one run.
- **Block:** `experimental.estimators.NestedSampling` (random-walk and slice-sampling replacement, multi-ellipsoid bounding, log Z with its uncertainty, posterior resampling).
- **Experiment:** `InferenceAndTesting` (evidence table).
- **Toy task:** Gaussian shells, a mixture of 2–5 Gaussians in 2–10 dimensions with known log Z, and Bayesian model selection between polynomial degrees on noisy data.
- **Baselines:** `AnnealedImportanceSampler` (AIS) and the wave 7 SMC sampler at equal likelihood evaluations; the harmonic-mean estimator (a known failure).
- **Expected metric:** |log Z error| and its reported uncertainty's calibration, mode coverage, and likelihood evaluations. Hypothesis: nested sampling and SMC are within 0.2 nats on unimodal targets; on separated modes nested sampling keeps every mode where AIS with few chains loses some; its log Z error bar covers the truth about 68% of the time; harmonic mean is off by several nats.
- **Source:** Skilling, "Nested sampling for general Bayesian computation" (Bayesian Analysis 2006); [arXiv:0809.3437](https://arxiv.org/abs/0809.3437)

### 12.5.12 Mutual-information estimators: KSG, MINE, NWJ, InfoNCE and SMILE (Kraskov, Stögbauer & Grassberger, PRE 2004; Belghazi et al., ICML 2018; Nguyen, Wainwright & Jordan, 2010; van den Oord et al., 2018; Poole et al., ICML 2019; Song & Ermon, ICLR 2020)
MI is central to representation learning, feature selection (12.1.13) and causal discovery but hard to estimate. KSG uses k-nearest-neighbour distances in the joint and marginal spaces; MINE trains a critic on the Donsker–Varadhan bound; NWJ uses the f-divergence bound; InfoNCE is low-variance but capped at log(batch size); SMILE clips the density ratio to trade bias for variance. The variational bounds' bias–variance trade-off is the main known result to reproduce.
- **Block:** `experimental.estimators.MutualInformation` (BINNED, KSG, MINE with moving-average gradient correction, NWJ, INFONCE, SMILE).
- **Experiment:** `InferenceAndTesting` (MI table).
- **Toy task:** correlated Gaussians in 1–20 dimensions with MI steps 2–10 nats (the Poole et al. staircase), and a cubic transform of the same.
- **Baselines:** the analytic MI; binned plug-in estimates.
- **Expected metric:** bias and variance of each estimate vs true MI and dimension. Hypothesis: KSG is best in low dimensions; InfoNCE saturates at log(batch); MINE and NWJ have high variance at high MI; SMILE has the best bias–variance balance; binned estimates fail above 3 dimensions.
- **Source:** [arXiv:cond-mat/0305641](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0305641), [arXiv:1801.04062](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04062), [arXiv:1905.06922](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06922), [arXiv:1910.06222](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.06222)

### 12.5.13 Goodness-of-fit and two-sample tests: the kernel Stein discrepancy, finite-set Stein discrepancy and classifier two-sample tests (Liu, Lee & Jordan, ICML 2016; Chwialkowski, Strathmann & Gretton, ICML 2016; Jitkrittum et al., NeurIPS 2017; Lopez-Paz & Oquab, ICLR 2017)
To test whether samples come from a model known only up to normalisation, the kernel Stein discrepancy needs only the score ∇log p: it is a U-statistic whose null distribution is obtained by a wild bootstrap. FSSD evaluates the Stein witness at a few optimised test locations, in linear time, and those locations show where the model fails. Classifier two-sample tests train a classifier to tell samples apart and test its held-out accuracy against chance; they are simple and interpretable for generated samples.
- **Block:** `experimental.estimators.SteinTests` (KSD with wild bootstrap, FSSD with optimised locations) and `experimental.estimators.ClassifierTwoSample` (C2ST with logistic or MLP classifiers, permutation p-values).
- **Experiment:** `InferenceAndTesting` (test table).
- **Toy task:** Gaussian–Bernoulli RBM null vs perturbed (the standard KSD benchmark, reusing wave 11's `RestrictedBoltzmannMachine` if it exposes scores, else a local Gaussian mixture), Student-t vs Gaussian; two-sample tests between a trained generator (wave 6 flows, read-only) and data.
- **Baselines:** MMD and HSIC tests from `KernelTests`/`KernelHypothesisTests` (needing samples from the model); the likelihood-ratio test when available.
- **Expected metric:** type-I error at α = 0.05 and power vs perturbation size and sample size; runtime. Hypothesis: all tests hold α; KSD has the highest power when the score is exact; FSSD is close at 10× less time; C2ST is weaker than MMD at small n and better at high dimension.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1602.03253](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.03253), [arXiv:1705.07673](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07673), [arXiv:1610.06545](https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.06545)

### 12.5.14 Randomised numerical linear algebra: randomised SVD, sketch-and-precondition least squares and stochastic Lanczos quadrature (Halko, Martinsson & Tropp, SIAM Review 2011; Avron, Maymounkov & Toledo, SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 2010; Ubaru, Chen & Saad, SIMAX 2017)
Randomised SVD multiplies by a Gaussian (or sparse sign) test matrix, orthonormalises the sample with a few power iterations, and computes an exact SVD of the small projection, giving near-optimal low-rank approximations in a few passes. Sketch-and-precondition (Blendenpik/LSRN) sketches a tall least-squares problem, factors the sketch with QR and uses R as a preconditioner for LSQR, converging in a fixed number of iterations regardless of conditioning. Stochastic Lanczos quadrature estimates tr f(A) (log-determinants, spectral densities, Hessian spectra) from a few Lanczos runs on random probes.
- **Block:** `experimental.randla.RandomizedSvd`, `experimental.randla.SketchedLeastSquares` (Gaussian, CountSketch and SRHT sketches; LSQR with preconditioning) and `experimental.randla.StochasticLanczos` (log det, spectral density, Hessian spectrum via Hessian-vector products).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.estimation.RandomisedLinearAlgebra`.
- **Toy task:** matrices with polynomial and exponential spectral decay (2,000 × 500), tall ill-conditioned least squares (20,000 × 100, κ up to 10⁸), kernel matrices for GP log-determinants, and the Hessian spectrum of a trained digits MLP.
- **Baselines:** `ThinSvd` (exact); normal equations and QR least squares; exact eigendecomposition; Hutchinson trace estimation (as in the FFJORD code) for traces.
- **Expected metric:** relative approximation error vs exact, and speed-up. Hypothesis: randomised SVD with 2 power iterations is within 1% of optimal rank-k error at 5–20× the speed of the full SVD; sketch-and-precondition converges in under 30 LSQR iterations at κ = 10⁸ where unpreconditioned LSQR does not; SLQ log-determinants are within 1% using 10 probes and 30 Lanczos steps.
- **Source:** [arXiv:0909.4061](https://arxiv.org/abs/0909.4061)

### 12.5.15 Gaussian process extensions: multi-output coregionalisation, heteroscedastic GPs and the GP latent variable model (Álvarez, Rosasco & Lawrence, FnT ML 2012; Goldberg, Williams & Bishop, NeurIPS 1998; Lawrence, JMLR 2005; Titsias & Lawrence, AISTATS 2010)
The intrinsic and linear models of coregionalisation share kernels across outputs through a coregionalisation matrix B, so a sparsely observed output borrows strength from others. Heteroscedastic GPs model the noise variance as a second GP (fitted by a most-likely-noise EM or variational methods). The GPLVM optimises latent inputs to maximise the GP marginal likelihood of observed data, giving a nonlinear probabilistic PCA (the Bayesian GPLVM integrates the latents with inducing points).
- **Block:** `experimental.ml.MultiOutputGp` (ICM, LMC), `experimental.ml.HeteroscedasticGp` (most-likely-noise EM) and `experimental.ml.GpLvm` (MAP GPLVM with back-constraints optional).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.probabilistic.GaussianProcessExtensions` (new class).
- **Toy task:** 3–5 correlated sine outputs with one output missing in a region; heteroscedastic 1-D regression (noise growing with x); GPLVM on a noisy 2-D manifold embedded in 20 dimensions (an oil-flow-like toy) and on digits.
- **Baselines:** independent GPs (`GaussianProcess`); homoscedastic GP; PCA and kernel PCA (`ComponentAnalysis`).
- **Expected metric:** RMSE and NLL in the missing region; test NLL of the heteroscedastic fit; nearest-neighbour class accuracy in the 2-D latent space. Hypothesis: ICM/LMC halve the RMSE in the gap; the heteroscedastic GP improves NLL by 0.3+ nats with equal RMSE; GPLVM latents give higher kNN accuracy than PCA on the manifold toy.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1106.6251](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.6251); Lawrence, "Probabilistic non-linear principal component analysis with Gaussian process latent variable models" (JMLR 2005).

### 12.5.16 Bayesian nonparametric latent structure: the Indian buffet process and the hierarchical Dirichlet process (Griffiths & Ghahramani, NeurIPS 2005; Teh, Jordan, Beal & Blei, JASA 2006)
The IBP is a prior over binary feature matrices with an unbounded number of columns: each object takes popular existing features and a Poisson number of new ones; a linear-Gaussian latent feature model with an IBP prior learns how many additive features explain the data by collapsed Gibbs sampling. The HDP shares a countable set of mixture components (topics) across groups through a Chinese restaurant franchise, so a topic model learns its own number of topics.
- **Block:** `experimental.ml.IndianBuffetProcess` (linear-Gaussian model, collapsed Gibbs with Metropolis steps for new features) and `experimental.ml.HierarchicalDirichletProcess` (direct-assignment Gibbs for topic models).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.probabilistic.NonparametricLatents` (new class).
- **Toy task:** the "cambridge" toy: 6×6 images made by summing 4 hidden binary patterns with noise (IBP); `SyntheticCorpus` documents from 5–15 planted topics with varying counts per group (HDP).
- **Baselines:** `TopicModel` LDA at fixed K (wrong and right K); `DirichletProcessMixture` (one cluster per object); NMF with fixed K.
- **Expected metric:** recovered number of features/topics, reconstruction error or held-out perplexity, and feature/topic matching accuracy. Hypothesis: the IBP recovers the 4 features where a DP mixture needs 16 clusters; HDP matches LDA's perplexity at the right K and beats it when K is misspecified.
- **Source:** Griffiths & Ghahramani, "Infinite latent feature models and the Indian buffet process" (NeurIPS 2005); Teh et al., "Hierarchical Dirichlet processes" (JASA 2006).

### 12.5.17 Manifold learning: classical MDS, Isomap, LLE, Laplacian eigenmaps, diffusion maps and PaCMAP (Torgerson, 1952; Tenenbaum et al., Science 2000; Roweis & Saul, Science 2000; Belkin & Niyogi, Neural Computation 2003; Coifman & Lafon, ACHA 2006; Wang et al., JMLR 2021)
Spectral embeddings preserve different structure: classical MDS preserves Euclidean distances, Isomap geodesic (graph shortest-path) distances, LLE local linear reconstruction weights, Laplacian eigenmaps graph smoothness, and diffusion maps diffusion distances at a time scale. PaCMAP optimises a layout with neighbour, mid-near and further pairs in phases and is reported to preserve both local and global structure better than t-SNE and UMAP.
- **Block:** `experimental.ml.ManifoldEmbeddings` (CLASSICAL_MDS, ISOMAP, LLE, LAPLACIAN_EIGENMAPS, DIFFUSION_MAPS, PACMAP), using `ThinSvd`/eigen helpers and `KnnIndex`.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.classic.ProjectionExperiments.manifolds` (new method; wave 6 rows unchanged).
- **Toy task:** Swiss roll, S-curve, a Swiss roll with a hole, noisy concentric spheres, and digits.
- **Baselines:** PCA and kernel PCA (`ComponentAnalysis`); t-SNE and UMAP (`NeighbourEmbedding`).
- **Expected metric:** trustworthiness and continuity at k = 10, Spearman correlation of geodesic vs embedded distances (global structure), and time. Hypothesis: Isomap unrolls the Swiss roll best (global correlation > 0.9) but fails with the hole; LLE is fragile to noise; t-SNE/UMAP win trustworthiness and lose global correlation; PaCMAP is closest to both at once.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2012.04456](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04456); Tenenbaum, de Silva & Langford, "A global geometric framework for nonlinear dimensionality reduction" (Science 2000).

### 12.5.18 Density-based and exemplar clustering: HDBSCAN, OPTICS, agglomerative clustering with Ward linkage, and affinity propagation (Campello, Moulavi & Sander, PAKDD 2013; Ankerst et al., SIGMOD 1999; Ward, JASA 1963; Frey & Dueck, Science 2007)
DBSCAN needs one density threshold; OPTICS orders points by reachability distance so that clusters at all densities appear as valleys; HDBSCAN builds the hierarchy over all thresholds from the mutual-reachability minimum spanning tree and selects the most stable clusters, with no ε to tune. Ward's agglomerative clustering merges the pair that least increases within-cluster variance; affinity propagation passes responsibility and availability messages to choose exemplars and the number of clusters automatically.
- **Block:** `experimental.ml.DensityClustering` (OPTICS, HDBSCAN with stability selection and GLOSH outlier scores), `experimental.ml.AgglomerativeClustering` (single, complete, average, WARD with Lance–Williams updates) and `experimental.ml.AffinityPropagation`.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.classic.ClassicMlShootout.densityClustering` (new method; existing rows unchanged).
- **Toy task:** blobs of different densities, moons with noise, nested circles, and digits embeddings; 500–3,000 points.
- **Baselines:** `KMeans`, `Dbscan` (best ε chosen on the labels: an optimistic baseline), `SpectralClustering`, `GaussianMixture`; `Diarization.agglomerative` (average linkage, read-only).
- **Expected metric:** ARI and NMI, noise-point precision, and sensitivity to the one hyperparameter. Hypothesis: HDBSCAN matches oracle-tuned DBSCAN on equal densities and beats it on varying densities, with min_cluster_size the only knob; Ward beats average linkage on blobs; affinity propagation finds a reasonable K on blobs and over-splits moons.
- **Source:** Campello, Moulavi & Sander, "Density-based clustering based on hierarchical density estimates" (PAKDD 2013); Frey & Dueck, "Clustering by passing messages between data points" (Science 2007).

### 12.6.1 Provably efficient tabular exploration: R-max, UCRL2, PSRL and optimistic Q-learning with Hoeffding/Bernstein bonuses (Brafman & Tennenholtz, JMLR 2002; Jaksch, Ortner & Auer, JMLR 2010; Osband, Russo & Van Roy, NeurIPS 2013; Jin et al., NeurIPS 2018)
R-max treats unknown state–action pairs as maximally rewarding until visited m times; UCRL2 plans in the most optimistic MDP within confidence sets (extended value iteration) and restarts episodes when counts double; PSRL samples one MDP from the posterior per episode and acts greedily in it; UCB-Q adds a count-based bonus to Q-learning with a specific learning-rate schedule and is the model-free counterpart. They have regret bounds that ε-greedy lacks, and the Deep-Sea/RiverSwim chains are where that shows.
- **Block:** `experimental.explore.TabularExplorers` (EPSILON_GREEDY_Q, RMAX, UCRL2, PSRL with Dirichlet–normal-gamma posteriors, UCB_Q_HOEFFDING, UCB_Q_BERNSTEIN), on `TabularMdp`.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.control.TabularExploration`.
- **Toy task:** `DeepSeaChain` (sizes 10–30) and RiverSwim (6–12 states), plus random sparse-reward MDPs; 30 seeds.
- **Baselines:** ε-greedy Q-learning; wave 11 `Rlsvi` (tabular) and bootstrapped DQN on one-hot states; count bonuses from `CountBonuses` (tabular counts).
- **Expected metric:** cumulative regret and episodes to first reward vs chain size. Hypothesis: ε-greedy needs exponentially many episodes in Deep Sea size while PSRL, UCRL2 and RLSVI scale polynomially; PSRL has the lowest regret, UCRL2 is the most conservative, UCB-Bernstein beats UCB-Hoeffding.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1306.0940](https://arxiv.org/abs/1306.0940), [arXiv:1807.03765](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03765)

### 12.6.2 Planning with a learned model in tabular RL: Dyna-Q, Dyna-Q+ and prioritised sweeping (Sutton, ICML 1990; Moore & Atkeson, Machine Learning 1993; Peng & Williams, 1993)
Dyna-Q learns a tabular model from real transitions and performs n simulated Q-updates from remembered state–action pairs after each real step; Dyna-Q+ adds an exploration bonus κ√τ for pairs not tried for τ steps, which recovers when the environment changes; prioritised sweeping orders the simulated updates by the size of the predicted value change, propagating new information backwards from where it arrived.
- **Block:** `experimental.explore.DynaPlanning` (DYNA_Q, DYNA_Q_PLUS, PRIORITIZED_SWEEPING with a priority queue and predecessor lists).
- **Experiment:** `TabularExploration` (planning table).
- **Toy task:** `GridWorld` mazes (Sutton & Barto's Dyna maze, the blocking maze and the shortcut maze, which change mid-run); `KeyDoorGrid` in tabular form.
- **Baselines:** Q-learning (n = 0 planning steps); wave 11 MBPO (`ModelBasedAndHierarchical`) is not used at this scale.
- **Expected metric:** steps per episode vs episodes, and recovery time after the maze changes. Hypothesis: Dyna-Q with n = 50 learns the maze in 5–10× fewer real steps than Q-learning; only Dyna-Q+ finds the shortcut; prioritised sweeping matches Dyna-Q with 5–10× fewer simulated updates.
- **Source:** Sutton, "Integrated architectures for learning, planning, and reacting based on approximating dynamic programming" (ICML 1990); Moore & Atkeson, "Prioritized sweeping" (Machine Learning 1993).

### 12.6.3 Options from the graph Laplacian: eigenoptions, covering options and a learned Laplacian representation (Machado, Bellemare & Bowling, ICML 2017; Jinnai et al., ICML 2019; Wu, Tucker & Nachum, ICLR 2019)
The eigenvectors of the state-transition graph's Laplacian (proto-value functions) describe its large-scale geometry. Eigenoptions are options whose intrinsic reward is the change along one eigenvector, so their policies traverse the graph's bottlenecks; covering options add an option that connects the two ends of the Fiedler vector, provably reducing the expected cover time. Wu et al. learn the Laplacian representation with a spectral graph-drawing objective from sampled transitions, which scales beyond tabular states.
- **Block:** `experimental.explore.LaplacianOptions` (exact Laplacian eigenvectors on tabular graphs, EIGENOPTIONS, COVERING_OPTIONS, LEARNED_LAPLACIAN with the graph-drawing objective).
- **Experiment:** `TabularExploration` (options table).
- **Toy task:** four-rooms and nine-rooms `GridWorld` variants, a 20×20 grid with walls, and `PointMaze` (continuous, for the learned representation).
- **Baselines:** primitive actions with ε-greedy; `OptionCritic` (wave 6); random-walk exploration; wave 11 `SkillDiscovery` (DIAYN) where it applies.
- **Expected metric:** cover time (steps to visit every state), steps to reach a far goal, and (learned) the cosine similarity of the learned representation with the true eigenvectors. Hypothesis: eigenoptions and covering options cut cover time 3–10× in multi-room grids; option-critic gives no exploration gain (as in wave 6); the learned Laplacian matches the first 3–5 eigenvectors with cosine > 0.9 on four rooms.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1703.00956](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00956), [arXiv:1810.04586](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04586)

### 12.6.4 Interactive imitation: DAgger and HG-DAgger, against behaviour cloning (Ross, Gordon & Bagnell, AISTATS 2011; Kelly et al., ICRA 2019)
Behaviour cloning suffers from compounding errors: the learner drifts into states the expert never visited. DAgger rolls out the current learner, asks the expert to label the visited states, aggregates them into the dataset and retrains, with no-regret guarantees; HG-DAgger lets the expert take over only when the learner is about to fail, which is cheaper in expert interventions. The aggregated dataset uses core `ReplayBuffer.relabel` (if landed) to rewrite actions with expert labels; the existing DAgger-style uses in `CachingWithPredictions` and `RapidMotorAdaptation` are unrelated tasks.
- **Block:** `experimental.explore.InteractiveImitation` (DAGGER with a β schedule, HG_DAGGER with an intervention rule, SAFE_DAGGER-style learned gating as an option).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.control.ImitationAndIrl`.
- **Toy task:** CartPole and the wave 10 `ContinuousMountainCar` with scripted or trained experts (a DQN/SAC from the repo); `GridWorld` navigation with a planner expert; expert-label budgets of 100–2,000 queries.
- **Baselines:** `BehaviorCloning` on the same number of expert labels; GAIL (`Gail`) and AIRL (`AdversarialImitation`) with expert demonstrations only.
- **Expected metric:** return vs expert labels queried, and expert interventions (HG-DAgger). Hypothesis: DAgger reaches expert return with 3–10× fewer labels than BC on CartPole and continuous mountain car; HG-DAgger needs fewer expert interventions than DAgger's full labelling at similar return; GAIL/AIRL trail both at this budget.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1011.0686](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686), [arXiv:1810.02890](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02890)

### 12.6.5 Inverse RL beyond tabular MaxEnt: apprenticeship learning, Bayesian IRL and deep MaxEnt IRL (Abbeel & Ng, ICML 2004; Ramachandran & Amir, IJCAI 2007; Wulfmeier, Ondruska & Posner, 2015)
Apprenticeship learning matches the expert's feature expectations by a max-margin or projection method, alternating with RL in the inner loop. Bayesian IRL samples reward functions from a posterior by policy-walk MCMC, under a Boltzmann-rational expert model, and returns uncertainty about the reward. Deep MaxEnt IRL replaces MaxEnt IRL's linear reward with a fully convolutional network on grid features, trained with the same state-visitation-difference gradient.
- **Block:** `experimental.explore.InverseRl` (APPRENTICESHIP_PROJECTION, BAYESIAN_IRL with policy walk, DEEP_MAXENT_IRL on grid feature maps), using `MaxEntIrl`'s soft value iteration unchanged where it is exposed.
- **Experiment:** `ImitationAndIrl` (IRL table).
- **Toy task:** object-world style grids (rewards a nonlinear function of nearby coloured objects, 16×16 to 32×32), with 8–128 expert demonstrations; transfer to a new layout.
- **Baselines:** `MaxEntIrl` (linear features, wave 6); `BehaviorCloning`; AIRL (`AdversarialImitation`).
- **Expected metric:** expected value difference (the return of the policy optimal for the learned reward, under the true reward) on the training and a transferred layout. Hypothesis: deep MaxEnt IRL beats linear MaxEnt IRL on the nonlinear object world; IRL methods transfer to a new layout where BC fails; Bayesian IRL is best with 8 demonstrations and the slowest.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1507.04888](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.04888); Abbeel & Ng, "Apprenticeship learning via inverse reinforcement learning" (ICML 2004).

### 12.6.6 Inventory control: the newsvendor with features, base-stock policies and deep RL with lead times and lost sales (Arrow, Harris & Marschak, Econometrica 1951; Ban & Rudin, Operations Research 2019; Gijsbrechts et al., MSOM 2022)
The newsvendor orders the critical-ratio quantile of demand; with features, Ban & Rudin fit that quantile directly by empirical risk minimisation (a quantile regression) instead of forecasting and then optimising. Multi-period inventory with lead times and lost sales has no simple optimal policy; base-stock and (s, S) policies are near-optimal benchmarks computed by dynamic programming on small instances, and deep RL (A3C/PPO) has been shown to match them.
- **Block:** `experimental.ops.Inventory` (NEWSVENDOR_ERM linear and kernel, PREDICT_THEN_OPTIMISE, BASE_STOCK and (s, S) by DP/search, a `rl` environment adapter for PPO), on `OperationsSims` demand.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.operations.InventoryAndPricing`.
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` feature-driven demand (seasonal, promotions, heavy-tailed) with critical ratios 0.5–0.95; single-echelon lost-sales inventory with lead times 1–4.
- **Baselines:** forecast-then-quantile with `SeasonalSeries` forecasters; core `Ppo` on the raw environment; the DP-optimal policy where the state space is small (oracle).
- **Expected metric:** average cost relative to the oracle. Hypothesis: newsvendor ERM beats predict-then-optimise when the demand model is misspecified; base-stock is within 1–3% of optimal at lead time 1 and loses 5–10% at lead time 4 with lost sales; PPO matches the best base-stock within the test budget only at short lead times.
- **Source:** Ban & Rudin, "The big data newsvendor" (Operations Research 2019); Gijsbrechts et al., "Can deep reinforcement learning improve inventory management?" (MSOM 2022).

### 12.6.7 Dynamic pricing with demand learning: Thompson sampling, constrained pricing and contextual pricing (Ferreira, Simchi-Levi & Wang, Operations Research 2018; den Boer, Surveys in OR&MS 2015; Javanmard & Nazerzadeh, JMLR 2019)
A seller learns an unknown price–demand curve while selling. Pricing is a bandit with a structured reward (price × demand): Thompson sampling over a parametric demand model (with an inventory constraint solved as an LP each period, Ferreira et al.) beats unstructured bandits; certainty-equivalent pricing with forced exploration is the classic; contextual pricing learns a linear valuation model from binary sale/no-sale feedback.
- **Block:** `experimental.ops.DynamicPricing` (CERTAINTY_EQUIVALENT with forced exploration, THOMPSON_PARAMETRIC, THOMPSON_INVENTORY_LP over `LinearProgram`, CONTEXTUAL_LINEAR_VALUATION).
- **Experiment:** `InventoryAndPricing` (pricing table).
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` linear, logit and constant-elasticity demand with unknown parameters, 5–20 price points, horizons 1,000–5,000, with and without an inventory budget; contextual valuations.
- **Baselines:** UCB1 and Bernoulli Thompson over discrete prices (wave 11 `StochasticBandits`); LinUCB (`ContextualBandit`) for the contextual task; the clairvoyant optimal price.
- **Expected metric:** revenue regret against the clairvoyant policy. Hypothesis: parametric TS has 3–10× lower regret than unstructured bandits; under an inventory budget the LP-based TS avoids early stock-outs that plain TS suffers; contextual linear pricing beats LinUCB over discretised prices.
- **Source:** Ferreira, Simchi-Levi & Wang, "Online network revenue management using Thompson sampling" (Operations Research 2018); Javanmard & Nazerzadeh, "Dynamic pricing in high-dimensions" (JMLR 2019).

### 12.6.8 Job-shop scheduling with learned dispatching: L2D, a GNN over the disjunctive graph trained by PPO (Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2020)
Job-shop scheduling assigns operations to machines to minimise makespan; dispatching rules (shortest processing time, most work remaining, first-in-first-out) are fast and weak, exact methods slow. L2D encodes the partial schedule as a disjunctive graph, embeds it with a GIN, and trains a policy with PPO to pick the next operation; it generalises to larger instances than it was trained on.
- **Block:** `experimental.ops.JobShop` (instance model, disjunctive-graph state, dispatching rules SPT/MWKR/MOPNR/FDD-MWKR, a small constraint-propagating B&B for exact optima on 6×6, and L2D with a GIN encoder and PPO).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.operations.SchedulingAndPacking`.
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` 6×6 (exact optimum known), 10×10 and 15×10 instances; train on 6×6, test on all sizes.
- **Baselines:** the priority dispatching rules; random dispatch; the wave 11 `BranchAndBound` is not reused (different problem) but the exact optimum is the reference.
- **Expected metric:** optimality gap of makespan on 6×6 and gap to the best rule or lower bound on larger sizes. Hypothesis: L2D beats every single dispatching rule by 5–15% on 6×6 and keeps an edge on 10×10; the gap to optimum stays 10–20%; MWKR is the strongest rule.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2010.12367](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.12367)

### 12.6.9 Online bin packing: First Fit, Best Fit, Harmonic, profile-based packing with predicted item distributions, and an RL packer (Johnson et al., SIAM J. Comput. 1974; Lee & Lee, JACM 1985; Angelopoulos, Kamali & Shadkami, AAAI 2022/JAIR 2023)
Items arrive one at a time and must be placed immediately. First Fit and Best Fit are 1.7-competitive; Harmonic partitions by size classes. With predictions of the item-size frequencies, Angelopoulos et al. pack according to a profile computed offline from the predicted distribution, with a robust fallback (hybrid with First Fit) that keeps a worst-case guarantee when predictions are wrong. An RL packer learns a placement policy from item and bin-level features.
- **Block:** `experimental.ops.BinPacking` (NEXT_FIT, FIRST_FIT, BEST_FIT, HARMONIC, PROFILE_PACKING with predicted frequencies, HYBRID robust variant, an RL placement policy with core `Dqn`), and offline FFD as the reference.
- **Experiment:** `SchedulingAndPacking` (packing table).
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` item streams from uniform, discrete (few sizes, the profile regime), and adversarial-for-FF distributions, 1,000–10,000 items; predictions with 0–30% error in frequencies.
- **Baselines:** First Fit and Best Fit; offline First Fit Decreasing (a near-optimal reference); wave 11's ski-rental/caching code (no direct reuse; the predictions-robustness pattern is the same).
- **Expected metric:** bins used relative to the FFD lower bound, vs prediction error. Hypothesis: profile packing beats First Fit by 5–10% on discrete distributions with accurate predictions, the hybrid degrades gracefully to within a few percent of First Fit at 30% error; the RL packer matches Best Fit on the training distribution and falls behind on shift.
- **Source:** [arXiv:2102.03311](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03311)

### 12.6.10 Routing metaheuristics and learned destroy operators: ant colony system, tabu search, a genetic algorithm with order crossover, and neural large-neighbourhood search (Dorigo & Gambardella, IEEE TEC 1997; Glover, 1989; Davis, 1985; Hottung & Tierney, ECAI 2020)
Wave 11 compared neural constructive solvers with 2-opt and nearest neighbour but no metaheuristics. Ant colony system builds tours from pheromone and distance heuristics with local and global pheromone updates; tabu search walks 2-opt/relocate neighbourhoods with a short-term memory forbidding recent moves; genetic algorithms with order crossover and 2-opt mutation are the classic population method. Neural LNS repeatedly destroys part of a solution and repairs it with an attention model trained for repair, searching at test time.
- **Block:** `experimental.ops.RoutingMetaheuristics` (ACS, TABU_2OPT, GA_OX, LNS with random/worst/related destroy, NEURAL_LNS with a learned repair policy), on wave 11's `RoutingEnv` instances (read-only).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.operations.RoutingMetaheuristics`.
- **Toy task:** `CombinatorialInstances` TSP-20/TSP-50 and CVRP-20 with exact or best-known optima (wave 11).
- **Baselines:** wave 11 `ClassicHeuristics` (nearest neighbour, 2-opt), the attention model and POMO (`RoutingShootout`, read-only), and the wave 11 learned 2-opt.
- **Expected metric:** optimality gap vs wall-clock time (anytime curves). Hypothesis: tabu search and ACS reach < 1% gap on TSP-20 within a second, beating the wave 11 neural constructors at equal time; neural LNS beats random-destroy LNS on CVRP at equal iterations; GA is the slowest to converge.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1911.09539](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.09539); Dorigo & Gambardella, "Ant colony system" (IEEE TEC 1997).

### 12.6.11 Matching markets: deferred acceptance and bandit learning of preferences (Gale & Shapley, AMM 1962; Roth, 1982; Liu, Mania & Jordan, AISTATS 2020; Liu et al., JMLR 2021)
Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance finds a stable matching (no pair prefers each other to their partners) that is optimal for the proposing side and strategy-proof for it. When one side does not know its preferences, it learns them from noisy rewards of being matched; centralised explore-then-commit and UCB with a stable-matching platform, and decentralised conflict-avoiding UCB, have regret guarantees relative to the agent-optimal stable matching.
- **Block:** `experimental.ops.MatchingMarkets` (DEFERRED_ACCEPTANCE both directions, stability checks, CENTRALIZED_ETC, CENTRALIZED_UCB, DECENTRALIZED_CA_UCB).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.operations.MatchingMarkets`.
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` two-sided markets with 5–20 agents per side, arm-preference gaps 0.05–0.3, horizons 2,000–10,000; serial-dictatorship and random-preference structures.
- **Baselines:** greedy matching on empirical means; random matching; the wave 11 `BanditPolicy` UCB per agent ignoring conflicts.
- **Expected metric:** per-agent stable regret and the fraction of rounds with an unstable matching. Hypothesis: centralised UCB has logarithmic regret, ETC is sensitive to the gap, decentralised CA-UCB reaches stability without communication at a higher constant; ignoring conflicts gives linear regret for some agents.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1906.05363](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.05363)

### 12.6.12 Game-tree search with learned evaluation: alpha–beta with move ordering and transposition tables, expectimax, and TD-Leaf(λ) self-play training (Knuth & Moore, AI 1975; Michie, 1966; Baxter, Tridgell & Weaver, ICML 1998)
Alpha–beta minimax prunes branches that cannot affect the decision; good move ordering (killer moves, history heuristic, iterative deepening) and transposition tables bring it close to the √b branching factor. Expectimax handles chance nodes. TD-Leaf(λ) trains an evaluation function by TD(λ) on the leaf positions of the principal variations found by search during self-play, which is how learned evaluations were first combined with deep search.
- **Block:** `experimental.games.MinimaxSearch` (NEGAMAX, ALPHA_BETA with iterative deepening, transposition tables and history ordering, EXPECTIMAX) and `experimental.games.TdLeaf` (an MLP evaluation trained by TD-Leaf(λ) self-play).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.multiagent.GameSolving`.
- **Toy task:** `ConnectThree`/`NInARow` (connect-four on 5×5/6×5 boards), `TicTacToe` (solved, for correctness), and a small stochastic game (a dice race) for expectimax.
- **Baselines:** `Mcts` with random rollouts and `AlphaZero` (wave 3–6, read-only) at equal wall-clock per move; a hand-written evaluation function.
- **Expected metric:** nodes searched to a given depth, match win rates and Elo (via `EloLeague`) at equal time per move. Hypothesis: ordering + transposition tables cut nodes 10–50× at depth 8; alpha–beta with the TD-Leaf evaluation beats MCTS with random rollouts on connect-four at equal time and loses to a trained AlphaZero; the TD-Leaf evaluation beats the hand-written one after a few hundred self-play games.
- **Source:** [arXiv:cs/9901002](https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/9901002); Knuth & Moore, "An analysis of alpha-beta pruning" (AI 1975).

### 12.6.13 Commitment and correlation: Stackelberg equilibria by multiple LPs and correlated equilibria by regret matching and LP (Conitzer & Sandholm, EC 2006; Paruchuri et al., AAMAS 2008; Hart & Mas-Colell, Econometrica 2000; Syrgkanis et al., NeurIPS 2015)
In general-sum games, a leader who can commit to a mixed strategy gets at least its Nash payoff; the optimal commitment is found by one LP per follower response (Conitzer & Sandholm), and security games (DOBSS) are the classic application. Correlated equilibria form a polytope given by linear incentive constraints, so the welfare-optimal CE is an LP; regret-matching dynamics converge to the set of (coarse) correlated equilibria, and optimistic Hedge converges faster.
- **Block:** `experimental.games.CommitmentAndCorrelation` (STACKELBERG_MULTI_LP via `LinearProgram`, SECURITY_GAME coverage, CE_LP max-welfare, REGRET_MATCHING to CCE/CE, OPTIMISTIC_HEDGE dynamics), on `MatrixGame` and random general-sum games.
- **Experiment:** `GameSolving` (equilibrium table).
- **Toy task:** chicken, battle of the sexes, Shapley's game, random 3×3–10×10 general-sum games, and a 5–10-target security game.
- **Baselines:** fictitious play and NFSP (`NeuralFictitiousSelfPlay`, read-only); CFR's regret matching in two-player zero-sum (`CounterfactualRegret`, read-only); a uniform-coverage defender.
- **Expected metric:** leader payoff vs Nash payoff, welfare of the max-welfare CE vs the best Nash, and the CE gap of dynamics over iterations. Hypothesis: commitment strictly helps the leader in most random games; the max-welfare CE beats every Nash welfare in chicken; optimistic Hedge reaches a 0.01 CCE gap 5–10× faster than vanilla Hedge or regret matching; fictitious play cycles on Shapley's game.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1507.00407](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.00407); Conitzer & Sandholm, "Computing the optimal strategy to commit to" (EC 2006).

### 12.6.14 Multi-objective RL: linear scalarisation, Pareto Q-learning and envelope Q-learning (Van Moffaert & Nowé, JMLR 2014; Yang, Sun & Narasimhan, NeurIPS 2019; Roijers et al., JAIR 2013)
With vector rewards there is no single optimal policy; the goal is the Pareto front or a coverage set. Linear scalarisation trains one agent per weight and can only reach convex parts of the front; Pareto Q-learning keeps sets of non-dominated Q-vectors per state–action; envelope Q-learning trains one weight-conditioned network with a Bellman operator that takes the best Q-vector under the given preference over all actions and sampled preferences, generalising across preferences.
- **Block:** `experimental.explore.MultiObjectiveRl` (SCALARIZED_Q per weight, PARETO_Q tabular, ENVELOPE_DQN weight-conditioned), with hypervolume and coverage metrics.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.control.MultiObjectiveRl`.
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` Deep Sea Treasure (a concave front) and a two-objective resource-gathering grid; a two-objective CartPole (balance vs energy).
- **Baselines:** scalarised core `Dqn` trained per weight (a coverage set from 5–11 runs); random policies.
- **Expected metric:** hypervolume, number of Pareto-optimal policies found, and adaptation error to unseen preferences. Hypothesis: linear scalarisation misses the concave points of Deep Sea Treasure, Pareto Q-learning finds the whole front in the tabular case; envelope Q matches the per-weight DQN coverage with one network and generalises to unseen weights.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1908.08342](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08342)

### 12.6.15 Restless bandits: the Whittle index, its learned versions and LP relaxations (Whittle, J. Appl. Prob. 1988; Weber & Weiss, J. Appl. Prob. 1990; Avrachenkov & Borkar, 2022; Nakhleh et al., NeurIPS 2021)
In restless bandits every arm changes state whether or not it is pulled, and a budget limits how many arms are activated each step (health interventions, maintenance, sensor scheduling). The Whittle index relaxes the budget with a subsidy for passivity and ranks arms by the subsidy that makes them indifferent; it is asymptotically optimal under indexability. When transitions are unknown, Whittle-index Q-learning (two-timescale) and NeurWIN-style neural index learning estimate the indices from experience.
- **Block:** `experimental.bandit.RestlessBandits` (WHITTLE_INDEX by binary search on the subsidy with value iteration, LP_RELAXATION policy, WHITTLE_Q_LEARNING two-timescale, NEURAL_INDEX with a small MLP), on two-state and few-state arms.
- **Experiment:** `experiments.bandits.RestlessBandits`.
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` restless arms: 10–100 two-state "adherence" arms with heterogeneous transitions, budget 10–20%, known and unknown dynamics.
- **Baselines:** myopic (greedy on current-state reward); round-robin; random; the exact optimum by dynamic programming on 4–6 arms.
- **Expected metric:** average reward relative to the optimum (small) and to the LP upper bound (large), and learning curves with unknown dynamics. Hypothesis: the Whittle policy is within 1–2% of optimal on small instances and within a few percent of the LP bound at 100 arms; myopic loses 5–15%; Whittle Q-learning approaches the known-model index policy within the test budget on two-state arms.
- **Source:** Whittle, "Restless bandits: activity allocation in a changing world" (J. Appl. Prob. 1988); Nakhleh et al., "NeurWIN: neural Whittle index network for restless bandits via deep RL" (NeurIPS 2021).

### 12.6.16 Bandits with knapsacks and budgets: primal–dual BwK and UCB-BwK (Badanidiyuru, Kleinberg & Slivkins, FOCS 2013 and JACM 2018; Agrawal & Devanur, EC 2014; Immorlica et al., FOCS 2019)
In bandits with knapsacks, each pull consumes resources and the process stops when any budget runs out, so the benchmark is the best distribution over arms (an LP), not the best arm. Primal–dual BwK keeps multiplicative weights over resources as prices and picks the arm with the best reward-to-priced-cost ratio; UCB-BwK solves the optimistic LP each round; the adversarial version uses a Lagrangian game. Wave 9's budget pacing is a contextual special case without learning of costs.
- **Block:** `experimental.bandit.BanditsWithKnapsacks` (PRIMAL_DUAL_BWK, UCB_BWK via `LinearProgram`, LAGRANGIAN_ADVERSARIAL), implementing `BanditPolicy` with resource consumption.
- **Experiment:** `RestlessBandits` (budgeted table).
- **Toy task:** `OperationsSims` arms with Bernoulli rewards and 1–3 resource consumptions, budgets B = 0.1T–0.5T, horizons 5,000–20,000; a dynamic-pricing instance with inventory (shared with 12.6.7).
- **Baselines:** UCB1 and Thompson ignoring budgets (wave 11 `StochasticBandits`); `BudgetPacer`'s throttling (wave 9, read-only); the LP optimum.
- **Expected metric:** total reward relative to the LP benchmark. Hypothesis: budget-ignorant UCB exhausts resources early and loses 20–40% of the LP value; primal–dual and UCB-BwK reach 85–95%; UCB-BwK is better at small budgets, primal–dual is faster.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1305.2545](https://arxiv.org/abs/1305.2545)

### 12.6.17 Evolutionary RL with step-by-step TD3/SAC drivers: ERL and CEM-RL (Khadka & Tumer, NeurIPS 2018; Pourchot & Sigaud, ICLR 2019)
ERL runs a genetic population of actors alongside a DDPG learner that trains from the population's shared replay buffer and is periodically inserted into the population; CEM-RL keeps a cross-entropy-method distribution over actor parameters, applies TD3 gradient steps to half of the sampled actors before evaluation, and refits the distribution, combining ES robustness with gradient efficiency. Both need TD3/SAC that can be driven one step and one update at a time, which wave 11 had to work around with a local `StepwiseTd3`. The enabling change adds `step`/`update` methods to `Td3`, `Sac` and `ModernSac` (their `train` loops call the same code, so existing runs are unchanged); `StepwiseTd3` may then delegate in a later PR.
- **Block:** `Td3`/`Sac`/`ModernSac` gain public stepwise drivers (`act`, `observe`, `updateOnce`); `experimental.explore.EvolutionaryRl` (ERL, CEM_RL, using `CmaEs`'s diagonal-Gaussian code where it fits).
- **Experiment:** `experiments.control.ModernOffPolicy.evolutionary` (new method; wave 10–11 rows unchanged).
- **Toy task:** Pendulum and wave 10 `ContinuousMountainCar` (deceptive reward), 10k–20k environment steps; populations of 5–10 small actors.
- **Baselines:** TD3 and SAC (the new stepwise drivers run in their standard loop); `CmaEs` and OpenAI-ES on actor parameters (`BlackBoxOptimizers`, read-only); wave 11's `StepwiseTd3` (identical results to the new driver as a check).
- **Expected metric:** return vs environment steps over 3–5 seeds, and success rate on mountain car. Hypothesis: the new stepwise TD3 reproduces `StepwiseTd3`'s curve; CEM-RL matches TD3 on Pendulum and reaches the mountain-car flag more often than TD3 or SAC (deceptive gradients); pure CMA-ES is far less sample-efficient on Pendulum.
- **Source:** [arXiv:1805.07917](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07917), [arXiv:1810.01222](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.01222)
